\chapter{Agitation}

\section*{Definition}
Agitation is a state of excessive motor or verbal activity, often accompanied by emotional distress, restlessness, and irritability. It is clinically significant when it interferes with daily functioning, care delivery, or safety.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Agitation arises from dysregulation in prefrontal-limbic circuitry, with reduced inhibitory control from the prefrontal cortex over the amygdala and hypothalamus. Excess dopaminergic, noradrenergic, and glutamatergic signaling, along with reduced GABAergic tone, contribute to heightened arousal and motor output.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Psychiatric:} Anxiety disorders, bipolar disorder (manic/mixed episodes), schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, PTSD
 \item \textbf{Neurological:} Dementia (especially Alzheimer's and frontotemporal), delirium, traumatic brain injury, epilepsy (ictal/postictal), Parkinson disease
 \item \textbf{Substance-related:} Alcohol/drug intoxication or withdrawal, stimulant overdose, caffeine excess
 \item \textbf{Metabolic/medical:} Hyperthyroidism, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, pain, infection, electrolyte disturbances
 \item \textbf{Medication-induced:} Corticosteroids, SSRIs (early treatment), anticholinergics, dopamine agonists
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}

\begin{itemize}
 \item Agitation is sudden in onset, severe, or accompanied by confusion, fever, or altered mental status
 \item You poses a danger to self or others
 \item Associated with head injury, seizure, or suspected overdose
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Irritability, restlessness, aggression, insomnia, pressured speech, psychomotor acceleration, delirium
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} Agitation is a nonspecific symptom that requires identification of the underlying cause to guide management.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Ensure a calm, low-stimulation environment
 \item Validate feelings, use a non-confrontational tone, and avoid power struggles
 \item Maintain regular sleep-wake cycles and avoid caffeine or alcohol
 \item Engage in physical activity to channel restless energy
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
 \item History from patient and collateral sources (onset, triggers, medications, substances)
 \item Mental status examination assessing for psychosis, mania, suicidality, and cognitive deficits
 \item Focused medical workup including vital signs, pulse oximetry, glucose, electrolytes, thyroid function, and toxicology screen
 \item Neuroimaging if new focal neurological signs, head trauma, or delirium of unknown cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Identify and treat the underlying condition (e.g., antipsychotics for psychosis, benzodiazepines for alcohol withdrawal)
 \item First-line pharmacotherapy for acute agitation: oral or intramuscular antipsychotics (e.g., haloperidol, olanzapine, ziprasidone) with or without a benzodiazepine
 \item Non-pharmacological: redirection, de-escalation, environmental modification
 \item In dementia-related agitation: behavioral interventions first; consider antidepressants (SSRIs), antipsychotics only for severe symptoms with careful risk--benefit assessment
\end{itemize}
