\chapter{Acute Bronchitis}

\section*{Definition}
Acute bronchitis is a self-limited inflammation of the tracheobronchial epithelium, characterized by acute cough (lasting less than 3 weeks) with or without sputum production. It is diagnosed clinically when you have an acute respiratory infection with cough as the predominant symptom, in the absence of pneumonia.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Viral invasion of bronchial epithelial cells triggers release of pro-inflammatory immune signaling proteins (IL-6, IL-8, TNF-$\alpha$), causing mucosal edema, increased mucus secretion, and ciliary dysfunction. Airway hyperresponsiveness and transient bronchial hyperreactivity may persist for weeks after the acute infection resolves.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Viral (90\%):} Influenza A and B, rhinovirus, respiratory syncytial virus, adenovirus, coronavirus, parainfluenza
 \item \textbf{Bacterial (rare):} \textit{Bordetella pertussis}, \textit{Mycoplasma pneumoniae}, \textit{Chlamydia pneumoniae}
 \item \textbf{Environmental:} Tobacco smoke, air pollution, chemical irritants, dust
 \item \textbf{Other:} Pre-existing chronic bronchitis or asthma with superimposed infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}

\begin{itemize}
 \item Fever greater than 101\textdegree{}F (38.3\textdegree{}C) lasting more than 3 days
 \item Cough producing blood-tinged sputum (coughing up blood)
 \item shortness of breath at rest, tachypnea, or oxygen saturation less than 94\%
 \item Symptoms persisting beyond 3 weeks without improvement
 \item Recurrent episodes of acute bronchitis (more than 2 per year) suggesting underlying lung disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Cough (dry or productive), sputum production, fever, fatigue, wheezing, chest tightness, sore throat
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} Antibiotics are not routinely indicated for acute bronchitis in otherwise healthy adults, as the vast majority of cases are viral in origin.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Increased fluid intake (water, warm tea) to thin secretions and soothe irritated airways
 \item Honey (1--2 teaspoons) for cough suppression (avoid in children under 1 year)
 \item Steam inhalation or humidifier to loosen mucus and relieve bronchial irritation
 \item NSAIDs or acetaminophen for fever and chest discomfort
 \item Avoid smoking and secondhand smoke exposure during recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Clinical diagnosis based on history and physical exam; chest imaging not routinely required
 \item Chest X-ray indicated if vital signs abnormal, hypoxia present, or pneumonia suspected (focal findings on auscultation)
 \item Nasopharyngeal PCR for influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2 during respiratory season if antiviral therapy is considered
 \item Pertussis testing (PCR or serology) if paroxysmal cough with post-tussive emesis or inspiratory whoop
 \item Spirometry or bronchoprovocation testing if recurrent episodes suggest underlying asthma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Supportive care and symptomatic treatment (antitussives, expectorants, bronchodilators) as needed
 \item Inhaled beta-agonists (albuterol) if wheezing or evidence of bronchial hyperreactivity
 \item Antivirals (oseltamivir for influenza) within 48 hours of symptom onset for confirmed influenza
 \item Antibiotics (azithromycin, doxycycline) only if pertussis, mycoplasma, or chlamydia is confirmed or highly suspected
 \item Cough suppressants (dextromethorphan, benzonatate) for sleep-disruptive cough; prescribe judiciously
\end{itemize}
