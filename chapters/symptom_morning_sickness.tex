\chapter{Morning Sickness (Nausea Gravidarum)}

\section*{Definition}
Nausea and vomiting experienced during pregnancy, typically beginning around week 6, peaking around week 9, and resolving by week 14-16, though symptoms may begin earlier or persist longer.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Rising human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and estrogen levels during early pregnancy stimulate the brainstem vomiting center. Other contributing factors include enhanced olfactory sensitivity, delayed gastric emptying, and gastroesophageal reflux. Psychological factors and prior history of motion sickness may increase susceptibility.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Normal pregnancy-related hormonal changes
  \item Elevated hCG levels (more severe in multiple pregnancies or molar pregnancy)
  \item Estrogen surge in first trimester
  \item Enhanced sense of smell
  \item Delayed gastric emptying
  \item Gastroesophageal reflux
  \item Multiparous pregnancy history
  \item Personal or family history of hyperemesis gravidarum
  \item Migraine susceptibility
  \item Psychological factors (stress, anxiety)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Severe vomiting causing dehydration (inability to keep fluids down), weight loss, or ketosis (hyperemesis gravidarum)
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fatigue
  \item Food aversions
  \item Weight loss
  \item Dehydration
  \item Dizziness
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Eat small, frequent meals (every 2-3 hours) and keep dry crackers or plain biscuits at the bedside to eat before getting up to prevent morning nausea.
  \item Avoid triggers: strong odours, fatty or spicy foods, and sudden movements; choose bland, starchy foods (crackers, toast, rice, bananas) when nauseated.
  \item Stay hydrated by sipping fluids throughout the day — cold, clear liquids (water, ginger ale, electrolyte drinks) are often better tolerated than large volumes at once.
  \item Try ginger (crystallised, tea, or capsules up to 1g daily) or vitamin B6 (10-25 mg every 8 hours) which have evidence for reducing pregnancy-related nausea.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pregnancy confirmation (urine or serum hCG) and dating ultrasound to confirm gestational age and rule out molar pregnancy or multiple gestation.
  \item Clinical assessment of hydration status (skin turgor, mucous membranes, orthostatic vitals), weight compared to pre-pregnancy baseline, and urine ketones.
  \item Basic metabolic panel and liver function tests to assess for electrolyte disturbances and exclude hyperemesis-related complications.
  \item Ultrasound is performed to confirm viable intrauterine pregnancy and exclude molar pregnancy which can cause extremely elevated hCG and severe nausea.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item First-line pharmacotherapy is the combination of doxylamine (antihistamine) and pyridoxine (vitamin B6), which is safe and effective throughout pregnancy.
  \item If symptoms persist, add promethazine, metoclopramide, or ondansetron under medical supervision after discussing risks and benefits in pregnancy.
  \item Hyperemesis gravidarum requires hospitalisation for intravenous fluid resuscitation, electrolyte repletion (especially potassium), and antiemetic therapy.
  \item Thiamine (vitamin B1) supplementation is given to prevent Wernicke encephalopathy in cases of prolonged vomiting with weight loss.
  \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
  \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
