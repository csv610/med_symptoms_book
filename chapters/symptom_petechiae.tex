\chapter{Petechiae}

\section*{Definition}
Small (1-3 mm), non-blanching, red or purple spots on the skin caused by intradermal hemorrhage from ruptured capillaries, indicating platelet or vascular abnormalities.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Petechiae result from bleeding into the dermis due to thrombocytopenia (low platelet count), platelet dysfunction, capillary fragility, or increased intravascular pressure. Non-blanching with diascopy distinguishes petechiae from erythema. Distribution provides clues: dependent areas (lower extremities) suggest vasculitis or increased pressure; widespread suggests systemic thrombocytopenia or coagulopathy.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Thrombocytopenia (ITP, chemotherapy, leukemia, bone marrow failure)
  \item Platelet dysfunction (aspirin, NSAIDs, uremia)
  \item Vasculitis (Henoch-Schonlein purpura, microscopic polyangiitis)
  \item Infections (meningococcemia, endocarditis, RMSF, viral)
  \item Coagulation disorders (hemophilia, von Willebrand disease)
  \item Trauma or vigorous coughing/vomiting (Valsalva)
  \item Medication reactions
  \item Fat embolism syndrome
  \item Sepsis or disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)
  \item Senile purpura (fragile capillaries in elderly)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Petechiae with fever, bleeding from other sites, neurological symptoms, or widespread rapid onset (possible meningococcemia or DIC)
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Easy bruising
  \item Bleeding gums
  \item Nosebleeds
  \item Fever
  \item Fatigue
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Do not use aspirin, ibuprofen, or other NSAIDs, as these impair platelet function and may exacerbate bleeding.
  \item Protect the skin from trauma: use a soft toothbrush and electric razor; avoid contact sports and activities that cause bruising.
  \item If petechiae are isolated to areas above the nipple line (face, neck, chest) after coughing or vomiting, they are likely benign Valsalva petechiae and reassurance is appropriate.
  \item Monitor for progression: draw a circle around the petechial area and note the time; expanding or new lesions warrant urgent evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Full skin examination: distribution, size, and morphology (petechiae versus purpura versus ecchymoses); mucosal examination for gingival or conjunctival petechiae.
  \item Complete blood count with platelet count (critical); peripheral blood smear to evaluate for platelet clumping (pseudothrombocytopenia), blasts, or schistocytes.
  \item Coagulation studies (PT, aPTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer) if coagulopathy or disseminated intravascular coagulation is suspected; von Willebrand panel if bleeding history suggests a mild bleeding disorder.
  \item Urgent referral to haematology if platelet count is below 30,000/mcL, evidence of DIC, or concerning features for meningococcaemia or acute leukaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item If medication-induced (aspirin, NSAIDs, anticoagulants): discontinue the offending agent and monitor for resolution; reverse anticoagulation if clinically significant bleeding occurs.
  \item Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP): first-line treatment with corticosteroids (prednisone 1 mg/kg/day tapering) plus IVIG 1 g/kg if severe bleeding or count below 10,000/mcL.
  \item Infectious cause: appropriate antibiotics (ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 hours for meningococcaemia) or antivirals; supportive care for sepsis.
  \item Suspected DIC or meningococcaemia: immediate hospital admission, blood cultures, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics, and haematology consultation.
\end{itemize}
