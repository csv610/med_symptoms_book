\chapter{Arm Pain}

\section*{Definition}
Pain or discomfort anywhere in the upper extremity from the shoulder to the hand, arising from musculoskeletal, vascular, neurological, or referred sources.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Arm pain mechanisms include nociceptive (musculoskeletal strain, fracture, arthritis), neuropathic (cervical radiculopathy, peripheral nerve entrapment), ischemic (peripheral arterial disease, thoracic outlet syndrome), and referred (cardiac ischemia presenting as left arm pain). The brachial plexus distribution and shared cervical nerve roots mean arm pain frequently originates from the neck.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Cervical radiculopathy (herniated disc, foraminal stenosis)
 \item Rotator cuff tendinopathy or tear
 \item Fracture (humerus, radius, ulna)
 \item Lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow)
 \item Medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow)
 \item Carpal tunnel syndrome
 \item Thoracic outlet syndrome
 \item Bursitis (olecranon, subacromial)
 \item Referred cardiac pain (left arm — angina or MI)
 \item Peripheral neuropathy (diabetic, alcoholic)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
 \item Arm pain with chest pain, shortness of breath, or sweating (possible cardiac), acute severe pain after injury, or arm pain with weakness or numbness
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Shoulder pain
 \item Neck pain
 \item Hand numbness
 \item Weakness
 \item Swelling
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important:} Your doctor will check for serious underlying conditions when evaluating persistent arm pain.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Rest the affected arm and avoid repetitive overhead, lifting, or gripping activities that aggravate the pain.
 \item Apply ice packs for 15–20 minutes several times daily during the acute phase (first 48 hours) to reduce inflammation.
 \item Take over-the-counter NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) for pain relief unless contraindicated by renal disease or gastrointestinal risk.
 \item Use ergonomic adjustments at work (keyboard tray, chair height, monitor position) to reduce strain on the arm and shoulder.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Your doctor will perform a systematic examination of the cervical spine, shoulder, elbow, forearm, and hand including provocative tests (Spurling, Neer, Phalen).
 \item X-rays of the cervical spine, shoulder, or elbow are obtained when fracture, arthritis, or degenerative disease is suspected.
 \item MRI or CT with contrast is indicated if cervical radiculopathy, rotator cuff tear, or occult fracture is suspected based on examination findings.
 \item Nerve conduction studies and electromyography (EMG) are arranged when carpal tunnel syndrome, cubital tunnel syndrome, or cervical radiculopathy is considered.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Physiotherapy focused on stretching, strengthening, and ergonomic correction is first-line management for most musculoskeletal causes of arm pain.
 \item Corticosteroid injections (subacromial, lateral epicondyle, carpal tunnel) provide temporary relief for tendinopathies and entrapment neuropathies.
 \item Surgical options include carpal tunnel release, cubital tunnel decompression, rotator cuff repair, or cervical discectomy depending on the underlying pathology.
 \item Neuropathic pain agents (gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline) are prescribed for radicular or neuropathic arm pain.
 \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
 \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
