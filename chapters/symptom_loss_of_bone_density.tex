\chapter{Loss of Bone Density (Osteopenia/Osteoporosis)}

\section*{Definition}
Progressive reduction in bone mineral density, leading to weakened skeletal structure and increased fracture risk, most commonly resulting from oestrogen deficiency after menopause.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Oestrogen inhibits osteoclast activity through receptor activator of nuclear factor-kB ligand (RANKL) regulation. Oestrogen binds to oestrogen receptors on osteoclasts, promoting apoptosis and reducing bone resorption. The rapid decline in oestrogen during menopause removes this inhibitory signal, uncoupling bone remodelling: osteoclast activity increases while osteoblast activity does not keep pace, leading to net bone loss. Trabecular bone (vertebrae, distal radius) is affected more rapidly than cortical bone. Peak bone loss occurs in the first 3-5 years after menopause.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Menopausal oestrogen deficiency (primary cause)
  \item Advanced age
  \item Low peak bone mass (genetic, nutritional, exercise-related)
  \item Calcium and vitamin D deficiency
  \item Glucocorticoid use (corticosteroid-induced osteoporosis)
  \item Smoking
  \item Excessive alcohol consumption
  \item Low body weight (BMI < 19)
  \item Family history of osteoporosis or hip fracture
  \item Secondary causes (hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, malabsorption, rheumatoid arthritis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Bone density screening (DXA scan) is recommended for all women aged 65 and older, or younger with risk factors; seek advice after any low-trauma fracture
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Back pain
  \item Loss of height
  \item Joint pain
  \item Fractures
  \item Muscle weakness
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Ensure a daily calcium intake of 1,200 mg through diet (dairy, fortified plant milks, leafy greens, tinned fish with bones) or supplements as directed.
  \item Maintain adequate vitamin D levels — aim for 600-800 IU daily from sunlight, diet, or supplements; higher doses may be needed based on blood levels.
  \item Perform weight-bearing and resistance exercises (walking, jogging, stair climbing, lifting weights) for at least 30 minutes on most days to stimulate bone formation.
  \item Avoid smoking and limit alcohol to no more than 1-2 standard drinks per week, as both accelerate bone loss.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
\item Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) of the lumbar spine and hip is the standard diagnostic test; T-scores define osteopenia ($-1.0$ to $-2.5$) and osteoporosis ($\leq -2.5$).
 \item FRAX fracture risk assessment tool (including age, sex, weight, height, fracture history, parental hip fracture, smoking, alcohol, glucocorticoid use, and secondary causes) guides treatment decisions.
 \item Laboratory testing (serum calcium, phosphate, 25-hydroxyvitamin D, PTH, thyroid function, testosterone in men, celiac serology) rules out secondary causes of low bone density.
 \item Referral to an endocrinologist or osteoporosis specialist is indicated for very low T-scores ($\leq -3.0$), fragility fractures despite therapy, or suspected secondary osteoporosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid) are first-line pharmacotherapy for osteoporosis, reducing vertebral and hip fracture risk by 40-70\%.
  \item Alternative agents include denosumab (RANKL inhibitor, subcutaneous injection every 6 months) or selective oestrogen receptor modulators (raloxifene) for women unable to tolerate bisphosphonates.
  \item Teriparatide (PTH analogue) or romosozumab (sclerostin inhibitor) are anabolic agents reserved for severe osteoporosis or those who fail or cannot tolerate other therapies.
  \item Adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation is essential as a foundation for all osteoporosis pharmacotherapy.
  \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
  \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
