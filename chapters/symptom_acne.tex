\chapter{Acne}

\section*{Definition}
A common skin condition characterised by the formation of comedones (blackheads and whiteheads), papules, pustules, nodules, and cysts, typically on the face, chest, and back, resulting from pilosebaceous unit inflammation.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Acne vulgaris develops from four key processes: increased sebum production driven by androgens (testosterone converted to dihydrotestosterone), follicular hyperkeratinisation leading to comedone formation, Cutibacterium acnes colonisation of the follicle, and subsequent inflammation. In perimenopause, declining oestrogen unmasks androgen effects, increasing sebaceous gland activity. Menopausal hormonal shifts can trigger late-onset acne in previously clear skin.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Hormonal fluctuations (perimenopause, menopause, puberty, polycystic ovary syndrome)
 \item Androgen excess (increased sebum production)
 \item Genetic predisposition to follicular hyperkeratinisation
 \item Cutibacterium acnes overgrowth
 \item Comedogenic cosmetics or skincare products
 \item Medications (corticosteroids, lithium, androgens,certain contraceptives)
 \item Stress (cortisol-mediated androgen stimulation)
 \item High glycaemic load diet (controversial, potential insulin/IGF-1 mediated)
 \item Mechanical occlusion (headbands, helmets, face masks)
 \item Excessive facial cleansing or scrubbing (irritation)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
 \item Acne that is severe, painful, cystic, causing scarring, or unresponsive to over-the-counter treatments for 2-3 months
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Oily skin
 \item Hair loss
 \item Hirsutism
 \item Irregular periods
 \item Skin changes
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important:} Your doctor will check for serious underlying conditions when evaluating persistent acne.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Use gentle, non-comedogenic cleansers twice daily and avoid harsh scrubbing or over-washing.
 \item Apply over-the-counter topical benzoyl peroxide or salicylic acid products to active lesions.
 \item Avoid picking or squeezing lesions to reduce scarring and prevent secondary infection.
 \item Use oil-free, non-comedogenic moisturisers and sunscreens labelled non-comedogenic.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Your doctor will examine your face, chest, and back to classify acne severity (comedonal, papular, pustular, nodulocystic).
 \item Hormonal evaluation including serum testosterone, DHEAS, and LH/FSH may be ordered if signs of hyperandrogenism are present.
 \item Skin swab for bacterial culture may be performed if secondary infection or gram-negative folliculitis is suspected.
 \item Referral to a dermatologist is indicated for severe, scarring, or treatment-resistant acne for consideration of isotretinoin therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Topical therapy with retinoids (adapalene, tretinoin) and antimicrobials (clindamycin, benzoyl peroxide) forms the first-line treatment.
 \item Oral antibiotics (doxycycline, minocycline) are used for moderate-to-severe inflammatory acne for a limited duration.
 \item Hormonal therapy including oral contraceptives or spironolactone is effective for women with hormonal acne.
 \item Isotretinoin is reserved for severe nodulocystic or treatment-resistant acne under specialist supervision with regular monitoring.
 \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
 \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
