\chapter{Vaginal Irritation and Infection}

\section*{Definition}
Inflammation of the vaginal mucosa characterized by itching, burning, abnormal discharge, odor, and discomfort, most commonly caused by infectious or irritant triggers.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Vaginal ecosystem disruption alters normal lactobacillus-dominated flora, increasing pH and allowing pathogen overgrowth. Common pathogens: Candida albicans (fungal), Gardnerella vaginalis with other anaerobes (bacterial vaginosis), Trichomonas vaginalis (parasitic). Non-infectious causes include chemical irritants, allergic reactions, and atrophic changes.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Vulvovaginal candidiasis (yeast infection)
  \item Bacterial vaginosis
  \item Trichomoniasis (sexually transmitted)
  \item Chemical irritants (soaps, douches, detergents, spermicides)
  \item Atrophic vaginitis (menopause-related)
  \item Allergic reactions (latex, lubricants, contraceptive devices)
  \item Poor hygiene or excessive hygiene
  \item Tight non-breathable clothing
  \item Diabetes mellitus (increased glucose promotes yeast)
  \item Antibiotic use disrupting normal flora
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Vaginal irritation with abnormal discharge, fever, pelvic pain, or recurrent episodes
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Vaginal discharge
  \item Itching
  \item painful urination
  \item Painful sex
  \item Abnormal odor
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Stop using any new soaps, detergents, fabric softeners, vaginal sprays, douches, or feminine hygiene products that may be causing chemical irritation.
  \item Wear breathable cotton underwear and loose-fitting clothing; remove damp exercise clothing promptly to reduce moisture that promotes yeast and bacterial overgrowth.
  \item Apply a cool compress to the vulvar area for 10--15 minutes to relieve acute itching and burning; avoid scratching.
  \item For suspected yeast infection (if previously diagnosed): clotrimazole 1\% cream or miconazole vaginal suppositories as per package directions; discontinue and see a doctor if no improvement within three days.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Speculum examination: assess discharge colour, consistency, odour, and amount; collect vaginal swabs for wet mount, pH, KOH, and saline microscopy.
  \item Vaginal pH testing: pH below 4.5 suggests Candida; pH above 4.5 suggests bacterial vaginosis or trichomonas.
  \item Wet mount microscopy: budding yeast and pseudohyphae (Candida), clue cells (bacterial vaginosis), motile flagellated organisms (trichomonads).
  \item NAAT for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomonas in sexually active patients; HSV PCR if herpetic lesions are present.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Vulvovaginal candidiasis: single-dose oral fluconazole 150 mg or topical azole cream (clotrimazole 1\% for seven days); recurrent cases require maintenance fluconazole 150 mg weekly for six months.
  \item Bacterial vaginosis: metronidazole gel 0.75\% once daily intravaginally for five days or oral metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for seven days; clindamycin cream 2\% as an alternative.
  \item Trichomoniasis: oral tinidazole 2 g single dose or metronidazole 2 g single dose; treat sexual partner(s) simultaneously to prevent reinfection.
  \item Atrophic vaginitis: topical vaginal oestrogen therapy (cream, tablet, or ring) as described in the vaginal dryness section.
\end{itemize}
