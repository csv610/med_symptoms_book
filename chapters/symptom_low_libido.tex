\chapter{Low Libido (Female Hypoactive Sexual Desire Disorder)}

\section*{Definition}
Persistent or recurrent absence or deficiency of sexual fantasies, thoughts, and desire for sexual activity, causing marked distress or interpersonal difficulty.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Female sexual desire involves a complex interplay of neurological, hormonal, and psychosocial factors. Testosterone (produced by ovaries and adrenals) is the primary hormone driving sexual desire in women; its levels decline with age and fall significantly after menopause. Oestrogen maintains vaginal health and lubrication; its decline causes dyspareunia, which secondarily reduces desire. Dopamine (reward/anticipation) and serotonin (inhibition) balance sexual motivation. Menopausal hormonal shifts tilt this balance toward inhibition, while sleep deprivation, fatigue, and mood changes further suppress desire.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Menopausal testosterone and oestrogen decline
  \item Psychosocial factors (relationship conflict, stress, body image)
  \item Depression and anxiety
  \item Medication side effects (SSRIs, SNRIs, antipsychotics, hormonal contraceptives)
  \item Vaginal dryness and dyspareunia (fear of pain)
  \item Fatigue and sleep disturbance
  \item Hypothyroidism
  \item Hyperprolactinaemia
  \item Bilateral oophorectomy (surgical menopause)
  \item Chronic illness (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, cancer)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Low libido causing personal distress, affecting relationships, or associated with depression, pain during sex, or other menopausal symptoms
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Vaginal dryness
  \item Painful sex
  \item Fatigue
  \item Depression
  \item Anxiety
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Make time for intimacy without pressure — schedule relaxed, non-goal-oriented time with your partner focusing on touch and connection rather than intercourse.
  \item Address vaginal dryness proactively with water-based lubricants and long-acting vaginal moisturisers to reduce pain during sex, which can lower desire.
  \item Prioritise sleep and stress management, as fatigue and cortisol elevation directly suppress testosterone and sexual interest.
  \item Consider couples counselling or sex therapy to address relationship dynamics, communication issues, and emotional barriers to desire.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Biopsychosocial assessment covering medical history (surgical menopause, chronic illness), medications (SSRIs, contraceptives), relationship context, and psychological factors.
  \item Hormonal workup: serum total and free testosterone, oestradiol, FSH, LH, prolactin, and thyroid function tests.
  \item Vaginal health assessment to identify atrophy, infection, or dyspareunia that may contribute to low desire.
  \item Referral to a menopause specialist, gynaecologist, or psychosexual therapist is arranged for complex or persistent cases.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Vaginal oestrogen (cream, tablet, or ring) and non-hormonal moisturisers/lubricants address physical discomfort that secondarily suppresses desire.
  \item Testosterone therapy (off-label in women in many countries) may be considered for hypoactive sexual desire disorder in postmenopausal women, using low-dose transdermal preparations.
  \item Switching antidepressants — particularly from SSRIs to bupropion or agomelatine — may improve libido if medication-induced sexual dysfunction is contributing.
  \item Psychosexual therapy (including sensate focus, couples counselling, and CBT) addresses the psychological and relational dimensions of low desire.
  \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
  \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
