\chapter{Lump Under Armpit (Axillary Mass)}

\section*{Definition}
A palpable swelling or mass in the axillary region, most commonly representing enlarged lymph nodes but also arising from breast tissue, skin lesions, or subcutaneous structures.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Axillary lymph nodes drain the upper limb, breast, chest wall, and upper back. Lymphadenopathy results from infection (local or systemic), inflammatory conditions, or malignancy (lymphoma, breast cancer metastasis). Other causes include sebaceous cysts, lipomas, hidradenitis suppurativa, and accessory breast tissue.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lymphadenopathy (reactive from infection of arm/hand)
  \item Breast cancer metastasis
  \item Lymphoma or leukemia
  \item Sebaceous cyst or epidermoid cyst
  \item Lipoma (benign fatty tumor)
  \item Hidradenitis suppurativa (inflamed sweat gland)
  \item Cat scratch disease (Bartonella henselae)
  \item Tuberculosis or mycobacterial infection
  \item Sarcoidosis
  \item Vaccination reaction (recent COVID-19, flu, or other vaccine)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Firm, fixed, non-tender lump, progressive enlargement, or lump with fever, night sweats, or unexplained weight loss
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Breast lump
  \item Arm swelling
  \item Fever
  \item Night sweats
  \item Weight loss
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Do not repeatedly poke, squeeze, or massage the lump, as this can cause inflammation or spread infection.
  \item Apply a warm compress to the area for 10-15 minutes several times daily if the lump is tender or appears infected.
  \item Monitor the lump's size, tenderness, and any overlying skin changes (redness, warmth); take a photo daily with an object for size reference.
  \item If you have recently received a vaccine (COVID-19, flu), axillary lymphadenopathy can be a normal reaction that resolves within 1-2 weeks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Clinical examination noting size, consistency (firm vs soft, rubbery), mobility (fixed vs mobile), tenderness, and overlying skin changes.
  \item Ultrasound of the axilla to differentiate cystic (sebaceous cyst, abscess) from solid (lymph node, lipoma) lesions and to guide fine-needle aspiration if needed.
  \item Full blood count, inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR), and screening for infection (EBV, CMV, toxoplasmosis, cat scratch serology) if lymphadenopathy is generalised.
  \item Excisional biopsy or core needle biopsy is performed for solid lesions that are suspicious for malignancy, progressively enlarging, or persistent beyond 4-6 weeks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Reactive lymphadenopathy from infection resolves with treatment of the underlying infection; warm compresses and NSAIDs provide symptomatic relief.
  \item Sebaceous cyst or lipoma — benign lesions may be observed or excised if symptomatic, infected, or cosmetically concerning.
  \item Malignant lymphadenopathy (lymphoma, breast cancer metastasis) requires tissue diagnosis (core biopsy or excisional biopsy) followed by oncology-directed therapy (chemotherapy, radiation, surgery).
  \item Hidradenitis suppurativa is managed with antibiotics (clindamycin/rifampicin), anti-inflammatory therapy (adalimumab), and surgical deroofing or excision for chronic lesions.
  \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
  \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
