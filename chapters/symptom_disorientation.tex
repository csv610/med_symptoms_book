\chapter{Disorientation}

\section*{Definition}
Impairment of awareness of time, place, or person, often accompanied by confusion, difficulty concentrating, and inability to perform familiar tasks.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Disorientation results from dysfunction in cortical and subcortical networks supporting orientation and attention. Acute disorientation (delirium) involves global cognitive impairment from metabolic, toxic, infectious, or structural causes. Chronic disorientation (dementia) reflects progressive neurodegenerative damage. The reticular activating system and prefrontal cortex are key affected regions.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Delirium (metabolic, infectious, medication-induced)
  \item Alzheimer disease and other dementias
  \item Head trauma or traumatic brain injury
  \item Stroke or transient ischemic attack
  \item Seizure (especially complex partial or post-ictal)
  \item Hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia
  \item Electrolyte imbalances
  \item Medication side effects (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids)
  \item Alcohol intoxication or withdrawal
  \item Severe dehydration or malnutrition
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Sudden-onset disorientation (possible stroke, hypoglycemia, or delirium — emergency evaluation)
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Memory loss
  \item Confusion
  \item Agitation
  \item Dizziness
  \item Speech difficulty
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Ensure a safe environment by removing tripping hazards and keeping familiar objects within reach.
  \item Maintain a consistent daily routine with regular meals, sleep, and activities.
  \item Use calendars, clocks, and notes to reinforce orientation to time and place.
  \item Keep the person calm and reassured; avoid overstimulation or sudden changes.
  \item Seek emergency medical care if disorientation is sudden or accompanied by other neurological symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will perform a mental status examination assessing orientation, attention, and memory.
  \item Blood tests screen for metabolic derangements, infection, hypoglycemia, and electrolyte imbalances.
  \item Brain imaging (CT or MRI) is often indicated to rule out stroke, hemorrhage, or structural lesions.
  \item An ECG and cardiac monitoring may be performed if cardioembolic stroke is suspected.
  \item Referral to a neurologist or geriatrician may be arranged for persistent or progressive disorientation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Treatment is directed at the underlying cause (e.g., antibiotics for infection, glucose for hypoglycemia).
  \item Environmental modifications and orientation aids help manage chronic disorientation from dementia.
  \item Medications for dementia (cholinesterase inhibitors, memantine) may slow cognitive decline.
  \item Delirium requires identification and reversal of precipitating factors.
  \item Regular follow-up is essential to monitor cognitive function and adjust the care plan.
\end{itemize}
