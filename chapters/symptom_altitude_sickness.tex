\chapter{Altitude Sickness}

\section*{Definition}
Altitude sickness, also called acute mountain sickness (AMS), is a group of symptoms that can occur when you travel to a high elevation too quickly without giving your body time to adjust. It typically occurs above 2,500 meters (8,000 feet).

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
As you climb to higher altitudes, the air has less oxygen. Your body normally adapts by breathing faster, increasing your heart rate, and producing more red blood cells over time — a process called acclimatization. If you ascend too quickly, your body cannot keep up with the lower oxygen levels. Blood vessels in your brain may widen and leak fluid, causing mild swelling. In severe cases, fluid can build up in the lungs (high-altitude pulmonary edema / HAPE) or the brain (high-altitude cerebral edema / HACE), both of which are life-threatening.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rapid ascent to high altitude (above 2,500 m / 8,000 ft) without gradual acclimatization
  \item Previous history of altitude sickness
  \item Physical exertion at high altitude before acclimatizing
  \item Sleeping at a higher altitude than the one you spent the day at (often described as "climb high, sleep low" — reversing this pattern increases risk)
  \item Individual susceptibility — some people are more prone regardless of fitness level
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
Altitude sickness requires prompt attention because it can progress quickly. Seek medical care immediately if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item You develop confusion, staggering, or difficulty walking (possible HACE — high-altitude cerebral edema)
  \item You have shortness of breath at rest, a persistent cough, or coughing up pink frothy sputum (possible HAPE — high-altitude pulmonary edema)
  \item Your symptoms worsen despite resting at the same altitude
  \item You are unable to walk a straight line or have trouble with coordination
  \item You become very drowsy or difficult to wake
  \item You have vision changes or severe headache unrelieved by medication
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Headache (most common symptom — usually throbbing and worse with exertion)
  \item Nausea and vomiting
  \item Dizziness or lightheadedness
  \item Fatigue and weakness
  \item Loss of appetite
  \item Shortness of breath with activity
  \item Difficulty sleeping
  \item Swelling of hands, feet, or face
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} Descending to a lower altitude (at least 500 m / 1,600 ft lower) is the most effective treatment for any form of altitude sickness. Never leave someone with altitude sickness alone — symptoms can worsen rapidly.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Stop ascending — do not go higher until symptoms resolve completely.
  \item Rest at your current altitude and avoid strenuous activity.
  \item If symptoms do not improve within 24 hours or get worse, descend to a lower altitude immediately.
  \item Drink plenty of water and avoid alcohol, sleeping pills, and tobacco.
  \item Take acetaminophen (Tylenol) or ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) for headache.
  \item Acetazolamide (Diamox) can help prevent and treat altitude sickness — discuss with your doctor before travel.
  \item Supplemental oxygen if available.
  \item A portable hyperbaric chamber (Gamow bag) can simulate descent in remote settings while you arrange evacuation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will ask about your recent ascent rate, the highest altitude reached, and your specific symptoms.
  \item They will check your oxygen saturation with a pulse oximeter and listen to your lungs for signs of fluid.
  \item A neurological exam (checking coordination, mental status, and walking) helps assess for brain swelling.
  \item A chest X-ray may show fluid in the lungs if HAPE is suspected.
  \item Brain imaging (CT or MRI) may be done if HACE is suspected and the diagnosis is uncertain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Descent is the definitive treatment — descending even 500-1,000 meters (1,600-3,300 feet) usually brings significant relief.
  \item Supplemental oxygen speeds recovery and is the main treatment for HAPE and HACE.
  \item Acetazolamide (Diamox) helps speed acclimatization and reduces symptoms.
  \item Dexamethasone (a steroid) reduces brain swelling in HACE and can be used for severe AMS.
  \item Nifedipine or phosphodiesterase inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) are used for HAPE.
  \item A portable hyperbaric chamber can buy time while awaiting evacuation.
  \item Prevention: Ascend gradually (no more than 300-500 m / 1,000-1,600 ft per day above 3,000 m), take rest days, and consider acetazolamide prophylaxis if you are prone to altitude sickness.
\end{itemize}
