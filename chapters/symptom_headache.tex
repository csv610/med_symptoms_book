\chapter{Headache}

\section*{Definition}
A headache is pain or discomfort anywhere in the head or face region. It is one of the most common medical complaints and can range from mild to severe. Headaches are classified as primary (the headache itself is the condition) or secondary (caused by another medical problem).

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Primary headaches such as tension headaches, migraines, and cluster headaches arise from changes in nerve signals, blood vessel activity, and muscle tension in the head and neck — not from an underlying disease. Secondary headaches are symptoms of another condition such as sinus infection, head injury, high blood pressure, or a more serious problem like meningitis or brain bleed.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Tension-type headache (most common): A dull, pressure-like band around the head, often from stress, poor posture, or eye strain
  \item Migraine: Throbbing pain, often on one side, with nausea, light sensitivity, and sometimes visual changes before the pain starts
  \item Cluster headache: Intense stabbing pain around one eye, occurring in clusters over weeks or months
  \item Medication-overuse headache: Rebound headaches from taking pain relievers too often (more than 10 days per month)
  \item Sinus headache: Pain and pressure over the sinuses, often with nasal congestion and fever
  \item Secondary causes requiring urgent evaluation: Head injury, meningitis, brain bleed, high blood pressure, or brain tumor
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item This is your first severe headache or the worst headache of your life (thunderclap headache)
  \item Headache comes on suddenly and reaches maximum intensity within seconds to minutes
  \item Headache gets worse over time or changes in pattern
  \item You are over 50 and develop new headaches
  \item Headache follows a head injury
  \item You have neurological symptoms: confusion, slurred speech, weakness, vision changes, or seizures
  \item Headache is accompanied by fever, stiff neck, or rash (possible meningitis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Nausea or vomiting
  \item Sensitivity to light (photophobia) or sound (phonophobia)
  \item Neck stiffness
  \item Visual changes — flashing lights, blind spots, or wavy lines before the pain starts
  \item Nasal congestion or runny nose
  \item Dizziness
  \item Pain worsened by coughing, sneezing, or straining
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} A sudden, severe headache (thunderclap headache) can be a sign of a brain bleed and requires emergency evaluation.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rest in a quiet, dark room during headache episodes to reduce sensory stimulation.
  \item Apply a cold or warm compress to your forehead or neck, whichever feels more soothing.
  \item Stay hydrated by drinking water throughout the day; dehydration is a common headache trigger.
  \item Keep a headache diary noting triggers (certain foods, stress, sleep changes, weather).
  \item Over-the-counter pain relievers (acetaminophen, ibuprofen) taken at the first sign can help, but do not use them more than 10 days per month to avoid rebound headaches.
  \item Maintain a regular sleep schedule and consistent meal times to prevent headaches.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will ask about the pattern of your headaches: when they started, how often they occur, how long they last, and what triggers them.
  \item They will ask about associated symptoms like nausea, light sensitivity, and visual changes.
  \item A neurological examination checks your vision, eye movements, strength, reflexes, and coordination.
  \item If a secondary cause is suspected, imaging (CT scan or MRI of the brain) may be ordered.
  \item Blood tests and sometimes a lumbar puncture (spinal tap) are done if meningitis or other infections are suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Tension headaches often respond to over-the-counter pain relievers, rest, and stress management.
  \item Migraines can be treated with specific medications (triptans) and prevented with medications, supplements, or lifestyle changes.
  \item Cluster headaches may require oxygen therapy, injectable or nasal spray medications for rapid relief.
  \item Medication-overuse headache is treated by stopping the overused medication under your doctor's guidance.
  \item Secondary headaches require treatment of the underlying cause (antibiotics for sinus infection, blood pressure control, etc.).
  \item Physical therapy, biofeedback, and acupuncture can help with chronic headache conditions.
\end{itemize}
