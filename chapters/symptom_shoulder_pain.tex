\chapter{Shoulder Pain}

\section*{Definition}
Pain or discomfort localized to the shoulder joint and surrounding structures, arising from the glenohumeral joint, acromioclavicular joint, rotator cuff, or referred from cervical spine or viscera.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
The shoulder's complex anatomy allows extensive range of motion but predisposes to instability and impingement. Rotator cuff muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) stabilize the glenohumeral joint; their dysfunction leads to impingement, tendinopathy, or tears. Referred pain from the diaphragm, gallbladder, or heart may present as shoulder pain via phrenic nerve connections.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rotator cuff tendinopathy or tear
  \item Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis)
  \item Shoulder impingement syndrome
  \item Acromioclavicular joint arthritis or separation
  \item Glenohumeral osteoarthritis
  \item Bicipital tendinopathy
  \item Labral tear (SLAP tear)
  \item Cervical radiculopathy (referred from neck)
  \item Fracture (proximal humerus, clavicle)
  \item Referred cardiac pain (left shoulder — angina or MI)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Acute shoulder pain after injury, inability to raise arm, fever, or chest pain radiating to shoulder
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Neck pain
  \item Arm weakness
  \item Limited range of motion
  \item Night pain
  \item Arm numbness
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rest the shoulder in a neutral position for 48--72 hours after acute injury; avoid overhead activities and lifting objects heavier than two to three kilograms.
  \item Apply ice packs to the shoulder for 15--20 minutes every two to three hours during the first 48 hours for acute pain, then switch to heat for muscle stiffness.
  \item Perform pendulum exercises (bend forward, let the arm hang and gently swing) to maintain range of motion without stressing the rotator cuff.
  \item Take NSAIDs (ibuprofen 400--600 mg or naproxen 250--500 mg with food) for five to seven days for pain and inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Focused shoulder examination: inspection for asymmetry, swelling, or atrophy; palpation for tenderness over the bicipital groove, acromioclavicular joint, and greater tuberosity.
  \item Range of motion testing (active and passive); assess for a painful arc (60--120 degrees of abduction suggests impingement) and drop arm test (rotator cuff tear).
  \item Special tests: Neer impingement sign, Hawkins-Kennedy test, Speed test (biceps), O'Brien test (labral tear), and apprehension and relocation tests for instability.
  \item Imaging: plain radiographs (AP, lateral, axillary views) for fractures, arthritis, or dislocation; ultrasound or MRI for rotator cuff pathology or labral tears.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rotator cuff tendinopathy or impingement: NSAIDs, physiotherapy with eccentric strengthening, subacromial corticosteroid injection if symptoms persist beyond six weeks.
  \item Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder): aggressive physiotherapy with stretching; intra-articular corticosteroid injection; manipulation under anaesthesia or arthroscopic capsular release if refractory.
  \item Acromioclavicular joint arthritis: NSAIDs, activity modification, intra-articular corticosteroid injection; distal clavicle excision for persistent pain.
  \item Full-thickness rotator cuff tear: surgical repair (arthroscopic or mini-open) for acute tears in active patients; conservative management with physiotherapy for chronic tears in sedentary individuals.
\end{itemize}
