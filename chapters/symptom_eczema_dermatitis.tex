\chapter{Eczema}

\section*{Definition}
Eczema (atopic dermatitis) is a chronic, relapsing, pruritic inflammatory skin disorder characterized by dry skin, erythematous papules, lichenification, and impaired epidermal barrier function. It affects 15--20\% of children and 1--3\% of adults globally.

\section*{Pathophysiology}
Eczema results from a complex interplay of epidermal barrier dysfunction (filaggrin mutations, lipid abnormalities), immune dysregulation (Th2-dominated inflammation with IL-4, IL-13, IL-31), and environmental triggers. Disrupted tight junctions and reduced ceramides allow transepidermal water loss and antigen penetration, perpetuating a cycle of inflammation, scratching, and further barrier damage.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Genetic:} Filaggrin (FLG) loss-of-function mutations, CARD11 variants, SPINK5 mutations
  \item \textbf{Environmental:} Allergens (dust mites, pollen, pet dander), irritants (soaps, detergents, wool), climate (low humidity, heat)
  \item \textbf{Microbial:} \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} colonization, \textit{Malassezia} species, viral infections (eczema herpeticum)
  \item \textbf{Immunologic:} Food allergies (egg, milk, peanut) in subset of children, asthma and allergic rhinitis (atopic march)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Widespread or severe rash interfering with sleep or daily activities
  \item Signs of secondary infection: weeping, honey-colored crusting (impetiginized), fever
  \item Painful, punched-out erosions or vesicles (suspicion of eczema herpeticum)
  \item Failure of over-the-counter moisturizers and low-potency steroids after 2 weeks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pruritus, xerosis, lichenification, excoriations, erythema, scaling, flexural distribution
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} The distribution of eczema changes with age --- facial and extensor in infancy, flexural (antecubital and popliteal) in children, and localized lichenified plaques in adults.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Emollient therapy: Apply fragrance-free moisturizer (cream or ointment) liberally and frequently, at least 2--3 times daily
  \item Brief lukewarm baths (5--10 minutes daily) followed by gentle pat drying and immediate moisturizer application
  \item Avoid known triggers: harsh soaps, scratchy fabrics, excessive heat, and stress
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Clinical diagnosis based on Hanifin--Rajka criteria: pruritus, typical morphology/distribution, chronic relapsing course, atopic personal/family history
  \item Serum IgE level and specific allergen testing (IgE or skin prick) for suspected food or environmental triggers
  \item Skin biopsy if atypical presentation to exclude psoriasis, cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, or contact dermatitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Topical corticosteroids: Class 6--7 (low potency) for face/intertriginous; class 2--3 (mid-potency) for trunk/extremities; class 1 (high potency) for severe lichenified plaques
  \item Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) as steroid-sparing maintenance
  \item Systemic therapy: Dupilumab (IL-4R\ensuremath{\alpha} inhibitor), oral corticosteroids (short bursts), cyclosporine, methotrexate for refractory cases
  \item Antihistamines for sleep disturbance due to pruritus; infection surveillance and treatment
\end{itemize}
