\chapter{Anaphylaxis}

\section*{Definition}
Anaphylaxis is a severe, whole-body allergic reaction that can be life-threatening. It typically begins within minutes to a few hours of exposure to a trigger such as a food, medicine, or insect sting. It most commonly involves the skin and mucous membranes plus either breathing problems, a drop in blood pressure, or severe gut symptoms. Because it can progress to airway blockage and shock within minutes, it is treated immediately even while tests are pending.

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
Use your epinephrine auto-injector and call emergency services immediately if any of these develop after exposure to a possible allergen:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Trouble breathing, wheezing, throat tightness, hoarse voice, or feeling that your throat is closing
  \item Swelling of the lips, tongue, or face
  \item Dizziness, fainting, rapid weak pulse, or a sense of doom
  \item Vomiting, cramping, or diarrhea together with hives and flushing
  \item Widespread hives plus any breathing or circulatory symptom
\end{itemize}
Do not wait for symptoms to worsen, and do not delay calling for help while you drive yourself -- have someone drive or call an ambulance.

\section*{How It Develops}
When your immune system has been sensitized to an allergen, antibodies called IgE sit on the surface of mast cells and basophils. On re-exposure, the allergen cross-links these antibodies, causing the cells to burst open and flood the body with histamine and other mediators within seconds. These chemicals make small blood vessels widen and leak fluid into the tissues (which drops blood pressure and causes hives and swelling), make the muscles around your airways and gut contract (causing wheezing, vomiting, and cramping), and stimulate mucus glands in the lungs. The same reaction can involve several systems at once, which is what makes anaphylaxis dangerous: swelling of the larynx can close the airway, and widespread fluid loss into tissues can cause shock.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Foods: most commonly peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, fish, shellfish, wheat, and soy
  \item Medications: antibiotics such as penicillin, aspirin and other NSAIDs, and chemotherapy or contrast agents
  \item Insect venom: bee, wasp, hornet, and fire ant stings
  \item Latex and some vaccines
  \item Exercise-induced anaphylaxis, sometimes triggered when exercise follows a specific food
  \item Idiopathic anaphylaxis, where no trigger can be identified despite a careful workup
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hives (raised, itchy welts) and flushed, warm skin
  \item Swelling of the lips, tongue, eyelids, or throat
  \item Wheezing, cough, chest tightness, and shortness of breath
  \item Nausea, vomiting, cramping, and diarrhea
  \item Dizziness, lightheadedness, and fainting from falling blood pressure
  \item Anxiety, a pounding heart, and a feeling of impending doom
\end{itemize}

\section*{Medical Evaluation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The diagnosis is made clinically, based on the rapid onset of skin, breathing, circulatory, or gut symptoms after a likely exposure.
  \item During the reaction, doctors check blood pressure, oxygen, and airways and may measure serum tryptase, a marker of mast-cell activation, ideally within hours of the episode.
  \item After recovery, allergy testing (skin prick tests or specific IgE blood tests) can identify the trigger.
  \item For unclear triggers, supervised food or drug challenges may be arranged.
  \item Your history is key: what was eaten or given, what was used, the timing, and which systems were involved.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Epinephrine injected into the muscle (usually the outer thigh) is the first-line treatment and should be given as early as possible.
  \item High-flow oxygen and close monitoring of breathing and circulation in the emergency department.
  \item Intravenous fluids to support blood pressure and reverse shock.
  \item Antihistamines and corticosteroids to help control skin symptoms and prevent late, recurrent symptoms; they do not treat the acute reaction itself.
  \item Bronchodilators such as albuterol for wheezing that persists after epinephrine.
  \item Observation for several hours, because a second phase of symptoms can occur after an initial recovery.
  \item Long-term prevention with immunotherapy (allergy shots) for insect-venom allergy, and strict avoidance of confirmed food and drug triggers.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Self-Care and Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Inject epinephrine in the outer thigh immediately when you suspect anaphylaxis -- do not wait to see how bad it gets.
  \item Lay flat with your legs raised (unless this makes breathing worse) to help blood return to the heart; do not sit or stand up suddenly if you feel faint.
  \item Call emergency services and tell them anaphylaxis and the time of the epinephrine injection.
  \item Carry two epinephrine auto-injectors, since a second dose is often needed if symptoms return before you reach help.
  \item Antihistamines do not stop anaphylaxis -- they are not a substitute for epinephrine.
  \item After recovery, ask for a referral to an allergist and a written anaphylaxis action plan.
\end{itemize}
