\chapter{Electric Shock Sensation (Formication)}

\section*{Definition}
A sudden, brief sensation resembling an electric shock, tingling, or crawling feeling on or under the skin, typically lasting seconds, and often occurring without an external stimulus.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Electric shock sensations, medically termed paresthesias, arise from ectopic impulse generation in peripheral sensory nerves or central sensory pathways. Oestrogen supports myelin integrity and nerve conduction; declining oestrogen during perimenopause may increase nerve irritability. Reduced progesterone (which has a membrane-stabilising effect) further lowers the nerve excitation threshold. Hormonal fluctuations alter ion channel function (sodium, potassium, calcium) in sensory neurons, leading to spontaneous depolarisation.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Perimenopausal and menopausal hormonal fluctuations
  \item Cervical or lumbar nerve root compression (radiculopathy)
  \item Peripheral neuropathy (diabetic, alcoholic, nutritional)
  \item Multiple sclerosis (central demyelination)
  \item Migraine aura (sensory variant)
  \item Anxiety and hyperventilation syndrome
  \item Medication side effects (antidepressants, anticonvulsants)
  \item Vitamin B12 deficiency
  \item Hypocalcaemia or hypomagnesaemia
  \item Transient ischaemic attack (sensory cortex involvement)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Frequent or persistent electric shock sensations with weakness, numbness, vision changes, or other neurological symptoms
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Tingling numbness
  \item Nerve pain
  \item Dizziness
  \item Anxiety
  \item Headache
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Identify and avoid triggers such as stress, fatigue, caffeine, or specific neck positions.
  \item Practice relaxation techniques including deep breathing and progressive muscle relaxation.
  \item Maintain good posture, especially when using computers or mobile devices.
  \item Apply gentle heat to tense neck or back muscles if nerve root irritation is suspected.
  \item Keep a symptom diary to track frequency, duration, and potential triggers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will take a detailed history of the sensation character, location, triggers, and associated symptoms.
  \item A full neurological examination assesses sensory, motor, and reflex function.
  \item Blood tests check vitamin B12, calcium, magnesium, and thyroid function.
  \item MRI of the spine may be ordered if nerve root compression is suspected.
  \item Nerve conduction studies and electromyography (EMG) evaluate peripheral nerve function.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Treatment is directed at the underlying cause (hormonal therapy for perimenopause, B12 supplementation for deficiency).
  \item Medications for neuropathic pain (gabapentin, pregabalin, tricyclic antidepressants) may be prescribed.
  \item Physical therapy and posture correction for cervical or lumbar radiculopathy.
  \item Stress management and anxiety treatment if hyperventilation or anxiety is contributory.
  \item Regular follow-up to monitor response and adjust treatment as needed.
\end{itemize}
