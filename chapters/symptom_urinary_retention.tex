\chapter{Urinary Retention}

\section*{Definition}
Inability to partially or completely empty the bladder, classified as acute (sudden and painful) or chronic (gradual onset with less discomfort but significant residual urine volume).

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Acute retention typically results from mechanical blockage (BPH, urethral stricture, stone, clot) or neurological causes (spinal cord injury, post-operative). Chronic retention involves detrusor underactivity or bladder outlet blockage with compensatory detrusor hypertrophy, leading to high post-void residuals, overflow incontinence, and upper tract damage risk.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) — most common cause in men
  \item Urethral stricture
  \item Medication side effects (anticholinergics, antihistamines, decongestants)
  \item Post-operative (anesthesia, opioids, pelvic surgery)
  \item Neurological conditions (spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, diabetic neuropathy)
  \item Severe constipation (fecal impaction)
  \item Bladder stones or tumors
  \item Pelvic organ dropping of an organ (cystocele) in women
  \item Prostate cancer or bladder cancer
  \item tight foreskin or paraphimosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Complete inability to urinate (emergency requiring catheterization), painful distension, or acute retention with fever
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Urinary frequency
  \item Hesitancy
  \item Weak urinary stream
  \item Overflow incontinence
  \item Abdominal distension
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item If you feel the urge to urinate but cannot start, try running warm water over your perineum, sitting in a warm bath, or applying gentle suprapubic pressure.
  \item Use the double voiding technique: urinate, wait 30 seconds, then attempt again to empty the bladder more completely.
  \item Avoid medications that worsen retention: antihistamines, decongestants, anticholinergics, opioid pain medications, and tricyclic antidepressants.
  \item Maintain regular bowel movements with adequate fibre and hydration; faecal impaction is a reversible cause of urinary retention, especially in older adults.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Post-void residual volume measurement (bladder scan or straight catheterisation): residual over 100 mL indicates incomplete emptying; over 300 mL is significant.
  \item Digital rectal examination to assess prostate size, consistency, nodules, and tenderness; pelvic examination in women to evaluate for cystocele or uterine prolapse.
  \item Urinalysis with culture to rule out UTI; serum creatinine and eGFR to assess for obstructive uropathy; PSA if prostate cancer is suspected.
  \item Uroflowmetry and pressure-flow studies to distinguish bladder outlet obstruction from detrusor underactivity; cystoscopy to evaluate urethral stricture, bladder stones, or tumours.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Acute retention: immediate urethral catheterisation (16--18 Fr Foley) to decompress the bladder; suprapubic catheterisation if urethral catheterisation fails.
  \item Benign prostatic hyperplasia: alpha-blocker (tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily) as first-line; 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor (finasteride 5 mg daily) added for larger prostates; combination for synergistic effect.
  \item Urethral stricture: endoscopic internal urethrotomy or open surgical urethroplasty depending on stricture length and location.
  \item Pelvic organ prolapse: vaginal pessary or surgical correction (sacrocolpopexy, anterior colporrhaphy); adjust oestrogen therapy for atrophic changes.
\end{itemize}
