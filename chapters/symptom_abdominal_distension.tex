\chapter{Abdominal Distension (Distended Abdomen)}

\section*{Definition}
Abdominal distension is a visible or measurable increase in abdominal size, often accompanied by pressure, fullness, or bloating. It may result from gas, stool, fluid (ascites), pregnancy, an enlarged organ, a mass, or a mechanical obstruction. Distension is a physical finding; bloating is the subjective sensation of abdominal fullness, and the two may occur separately. Abdominal enlargement from weight gain or pregnancy is not always gastrointestinal distension.

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
Seek emergency care for sudden or rapidly worsening distension with severe or continuous pain, a rigid or very tender abdomen, repeated vomiting or inability to keep fluids down, inability to pass stool or gas especially with vomiting, fever with severe illness or confusion, fainting, bloody or black stool, breathing difficulty, or a painful hernia that cannot be pushed back. Recent abdominal surgery followed by these symptoms also requires urgent assessment. If you are pregnant or could be pregnant and have significant pain, bleeding, fainting, or persistent vomiting, seek urgent medical care.

Prompt medical assessment is also needed for new, persistent, or progressively increasing distension; unexplained weight loss or loss of appetite; jaundice; recurrent vomiting; severe or persistent diarrhea; early satiety; or swelling that may represent new ascites.

\section*{How It Develops}
Abdominal distension occurs when the abdominal cavity or gastrointestinal tract contains more volume than usual, or when the abdominal wall and diaphragm respond abnormally to normal intestinal contents. Gas, retained stool, impaired stomach or intestinal emptying, ascites, inflammation, and obstruction are important mechanisms. In functional gastrointestinal disorders, altered gut--brain signaling and increased sensitivity to normal intestinal contents can cause prominent bloating or distension without a large increase in gas. In extreme cases, severe distension can raise intra-abdominal pressure and impair venous return, breathing, or intestinal blood flow.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Common functional causes include swallowed air, constipation, dietary fermentation, carbohydrate intolerance, functional abdominal bloating or distension, functional dyspepsia, and irritable bowel syndrome.
  \item Gastrointestinal causes include ileus, intestinal obstruction, intestinal pseudo-obstruction, abdominal adhesions, gastroparesis, inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, small-intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and gastroenteritis.
  \item Fluid accumulation (ascites) may result from cirrhosis, heart failure, cancer, pancreatic disease, or, less commonly, infection such as tuberculosis.
  \item Pregnancy, premenstrual symptoms, uterine fibroids, ovarian cysts or masses, enlarged organs, and other abdominal or pelvic masses can increase abdominal size. Colorectal, ovarian, and other cancers may present with persistent or progressive distension.
  \item Obesity and weight gain can enlarge the abdomen without causing gastrointestinal distension.
  \item Medicines can cause constipation or slow gastrointestinal movement. Examples include opioids, anticholinergic medicines, some calcium-channel blockers, iron preparations, and some medicines used for diabetes or weight management. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without medical advice.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Abdominal pain, cramping, early satiety, reduced appetite, nausea, or vomiting
  \item Constipation, diarrhea, increased gas, inability to pass gas, or a change in bowel habits or stool caliber
  \item Shortness of breath, ankle swelling, jaundice, or easy bruising when fluid accumulation or liver disease is present
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} Distension together with vomiting and inability to pass gas or stool can indicate intestinal obstruction. Do not take repeated laxatives or force food and fluids while awaiting medical advice in that situation.

\section*{Medical Evaluation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item History covers onset and progression; pain; vomiting; bowel and urinary function; diet; medications and supplements; menstrual or pregnancy status; weight and appetite changes; liver, heart, kidney, or gastrointestinal disease; family history; and prior abdominal surgery or hernia.
  \item Examination includes vital signs and assessment of hydration, abdominal enlargement, tenderness, guarding or rigidity, tympany, bowel sounds, hernias, masses, organ enlargement, and signs of ascites or shock. A rectal or pelvic examination may be appropriate for selected symptoms.
  \item Tests may include a complete blood count, electrolytes, kidney function, liver tests, albumin, lipase, urinalysis, pregnancy testing, inflammatory markers, or stool testing when indicated. New clinically apparent ascites generally requires diagnostic paracentesis to determine its cause and assess for infection.
  \item Imaging is selected according to the suspected cause. Ultrasound is useful for ascites, hepatobiliary disease, pelvic conditions, and pregnancy. CT of the abdomen and pelvis is often preferred for suspected obstruction, inflammation, mass, or acute intra-abdominal disease; plain radiography has a limited, selective role. Endoscopy or colonoscopy may be needed for persistent symptoms, bleeding, anemia, or other alarm features.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
Treatment addresses the cause and may include constipation management, dietary modification, treatment of an identified infection or inflammatory disorder, treatment of gastroparesis or another motility disorder, or management of an underlying mass or cancer. Ascites may require dietary sodium restriction, medicines such as diuretics, or therapeutic paracentesis under clinical supervision. Suspected obstruction requires hospital assessment and may require bowel rest, intravenous fluids, nasogastric decompression, endoscopic treatment, or surgery; surgery or another procedure may be needed when there is ischemia, perforation, strangulation, or an obstructing structural lesion. Persistent unexplained distension warrants follow-up rather than repeated symptomatic treatment alone.

\section*{Self-Care and Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item For mild, intermittent symptoms without warning signs, eat slowly, avoid carbonated drinks, and record foods that reproducibly trigger symptoms.
  \item Maintain ordinary hydration and physical activity unless a clinician has prescribed fluid or activity restrictions. If constipation is present, increase dietary fiber gradually; soluble fiber such as psyllium may be better tolerated than large amounts of insoluble fiber.
  \item Do not force food or fluids when repeated vomiting or obstruction is suspected; seek urgent medical advice instead.
  \item Avoid repeated laxatives, restrictive diets, or over-the-counter supplements without discussing the cause with a clinician. A low-FODMAP diet, if considered for suspected IBS, should be time-limited and preferably guided by a dietitian, particularly if there is a history of an eating disorder or risk of malnutrition.
  \item Do not stop prescribed medicines without medical advice.
\end{itemize}
