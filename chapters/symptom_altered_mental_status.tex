\chapter{Altered Mental Status}

\section*{Definition}
Altered mental status is a change in attention, awareness, thinking, behavior, or level of consciousness. It may develop suddenly or over hours to days. It is a symptom -- not a disease. Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance in attention and awareness caused by an underlying medical problem; it is different from a chronic, stable memory disorder such as dementia, although the two can occur together. Reduced consciousness ranges from drowsiness to deep unresponsiveness. A sudden or unexplained change requires urgent medical assessment because the cause may be a stroke, low blood sugar, infection, poisoning, or another life-threatening problem.

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
Call for emergency help if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A person suddenly cannot be awakened, is hard to rouse, or stops answering sensibly
  \item The change is sudden or the person has a seizure, facial drooping, one-sided weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, sudden vision or balance problems, or a severe headache
  \item Confusion is accompanied by a fever and a stiff neck (possible meningitis)
  \item The person has diabetes and possible low blood sugar, has taken too much medicine or an unknown substance, or is taking blood-thinning medicine and has fallen
  \item There is head injury, repeated vomiting, severe breathing difficulty, or rapid deterioration over hours
\end{itemize}
Treat this like a medical emergency -- do not wait to see if it passes.

\section*{How It Develops}
Your brain depends on a continuous supply of oxygen and glucose and a stable chemical environment. Illness, medication effects, withdrawal, toxins, dehydration, sleep disruption, pain, or an unfamiliar environment can disturb the brain's attention and arousal systems. Delirium often fluctuates and may be agitated (hyperactive), sleepy and withdrawn (hypoactive), or a mixture of both. Low oxygen, high carbon dioxide, low blood sugar, organ failure, extreme temperatures, seizures, stroke, bleeding, or head injury can reduce consciousness. A psychiatric illness can resemble confusion, but a new change should be treated as a medical problem until dangerous physical causes have been assessed.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Infections: urinary tract, chest, or blood infections; meningitis and encephalitis; and sepsis
  \item Metabolic derangements: low blood sugar, low oxygen or high carbon dioxide, dehydration, very low or high sodium, calcium, or ammonia, and liver or kidney failure
  \item Stroke or bleeding in the brain, especially with a new focal neurologic deficit; a transient ischemic attack can cause brief focal symptoms but usually does not explain ongoing confusion
  \item Head injury, including concussion
  \item Medications and substances: sedatives, opioids, anticholinergics, steroids, recreational drugs, and poisonings; alcohol intoxication or withdrawal
  \item Seizures, including subtle or non-convulsive seizures
  \item Cardiorespiratory problems that reduce oxygen delivery or increase carbon dioxide, including heart attack, heart failure, and severe lung disease
  \item Nutritional or endocrine problems, including thiamine deficiency, thyroid disease, and adrenal disorders
  \item In older people, even a mild infection or a medication change can trigger delirium
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Difficulty paying attention or following a conversation
  \item Disorientation to time, place, or people
  \item Wandering, restlessness, or agitation; conversely, sleepiness and withdrawal
  \item Hallucinations (often visual in delirium), suspiciousness, or unusual behavior
  \item Slurred speech, tremors, or jerking movements
  \item Fever, stiff neck, headache, or light sensitivity
  \item Cold, clammy skin or sweating with confusion (low blood sugar)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Medical Evaluation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Immediate assessment of the airway, breathing, circulation, vital signs, oxygen saturation, temperature, and bedside blood glucose, followed by a focused neurologic examination.
  \item A check of alertness, orientation, attention, and recent change from baseline using a tool such as the Glasgow Coma Scale, 4AT, or Confusion Assessment Method. A family member or carer may provide essential information.
  \item A careful medication, alcohol, and substance history, including recent additions, dose changes, missed medicines, or possible overdose.
  \item Blood tests selected for the presentation, often including a blood count, glucose, electrolytes, calcium, kidney and liver function, and tests for infection or other causes. An ECG and urinalysis or cultures may be appropriate.
  \item Brain imaging when there is a focal neurologic deficit, head injury, anticoagulant use, severe headache, or another indication for bleeding, stroke, or mass effect. CT is often the first urgent test; MRI or vascular imaging is used selectively.
  \item An EEG for suspected or unexplained persistent seizure activity. A lumbar puncture may be needed for suspected meningitis or encephalitis, usually after assessment for increased intracranial pressure and other contraindications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Correct the underlying trigger: glucose for hypoglycemia, oxygen when hypoxemia is present, fluids when appropriate, antibiotics for a confirmed or strongly suspected bacterial infection, and antidotes or supportive care for poisons.
  \item Review medicines and make clinician-guided changes, especially to sedatives, anticholinergics, and other drugs that can impair cognition; medicines that cause dependence should not be stopped abruptly without medical advice.
  \item Give naloxone for suspected opioid overdose when available and follow emergency instructions. Give thiamine promptly to people at risk of severe thiamine deficiency, but do not delay glucose when hypoglycemia is present; follow local clinical protocols.
  \item Treat seizures with anti-seizure medicines when a seizure is the cause.
  \item For severe agitation that causes distress or immediate danger, a clinician may use a short course of an antipsychotic after non-drug measures fail. These medicines are not routine treatment for delirium and require caution, particularly in Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia.
  \item Non-drug care matters most: treat pain, maintain hydration and nutrition when safe, provide glasses and hearing aids, use familiar people and surroundings, promote daytime activity and nighttime sleep, reorient the person, and prevent falls and pressure injuries.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Self-Care and Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item A new or worsening change in mental status should not be managed at home. Call emergency services and stay with the person; do not let them drive or wander.
  \item Do not give food, drink, or oral medicine to anyone who is drowsy, vomiting, seizing, or unable to swallow -- they could choke. If they are unconscious but breathing, place them on their side if safe and follow emergency-dispatch instructions.
  \item If diabetes is present, the person is awake, and they can swallow safely, check blood glucose. For a reading at or below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L), follow the 15-15 rule or local instructions; call emergency services if they cannot swallow, become unconscious, or do not improve.
  \item For suspected opioid overdose, administer naloxone if available, call emergency services, and provide rescue breathing or CPR if trained and instructed.
  \item Keep the person calm and gently reorient them. Gather the medicine list, any substances taken, allergies, medical conditions, and the time the change began for the emergency team.
  \item Never try to "sleep off" a sudden change in mental status -- get medical attention.
\end{itemize}
