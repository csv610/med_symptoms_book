\chapter{Nausea}

\section*{Definition}
Nausea is that queasy, uneasy feeling in your stomach that often comes before vomiting. It is not a disease itself but a symptom that can be caused by many different conditions — from a simple stomach bug to more complex medical issues.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Nausea is controlled by the vomiting center in your brainstem. It can be triggered by signals from your digestive tract (irritation or infection), your inner ear (motion sickness), your brain (increased pressure, migraine), or even by chemicals in your blood (medications, metabolic changes). When activated, this center sends signals that prepare your body to vomit.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Digestive issues:} Viral gastroenteritis (stomach flu), food poisoning, gastritis, gastroesophageal reflux (GERD)
  \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} Morning sickness, especially in the first trimester
  \item \textbf{Medications:} Chemotherapy drugs, opioids, antibiotics, anesthesia
  \item \textbf{Motion sickness:} Car, boat, plane, or amusement park rides
  \item \textbf{Migraine:} Nausea often accompanies migraine headaches
  \item \textbf{Inner ear disorders:} Labyrinthitis, Meniere's disease
  \item \textbf{Serious causes:} Appendicitis, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, increased intracranial pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Nausea lasts more than 48 hours without relief
  \item You cannot keep any fluids down and show signs of dehydration (dry mouth, dark urine, dizziness)
  \item You vomit blood or what looks like coffee grounds
  \item You have severe abdominal pain, fever, or stiff neck along with nausea
  \item You have a severe headache or confusion with nausea
  \item You have diabetes or a weakened immune system and develop nausea
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Vomiting
  \item Loss of appetite
  \item Abdominal pain or cramping
  \item Bloating
  \item Dizziness
  \item Sweating
  \item Increased salivation
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} Nausea that will not go away or comes with other concerning symptoms needs medical evaluation to find the underlying cause.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Sip clear fluids slowly — water, clear broth, or oral rehydration solutions. Ice chips can also help.
  \item Eat small, bland meals like crackers, toast, bananas, and rice. Avoid greasy, spicy, or strong-smelling foods.
  \item Ginger tea or ginger candies may help settle your stomach. Peppermint tea can also be soothing.
  \item Rest in an upright position and avoid sudden movements.
  \item Over-the-counter motion sickness medication (dramamine) or anti-nausea medications (meclizine) may help — follow label instructions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will ask when the nausea started, what triggers it, and what makes it better or worse.
  \item They will ask about other symptoms like vomiting, abdominal pain, fever, headache, or dizziness.
  \item They will check your vital signs and examine your abdomen for tenderness or swelling.
  \item Depending on your symptoms, tests may include blood work, urine tests, or imaging like abdominal X-ray, ultrasound, or CT scan.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item For mild nausea: dietary changes, ginger, and anti-nausea medications available over the counter.
  \item For persistent nausea: prescription anti-nausea medications (ondansetron, promethazine) may be needed.
  \item Treating the underlying cause is key — antibiotics for infection, migraine medications for headache, or adjusting medications that cause nausea.
  \item If dehydrated, you may need IV fluids. Severe vomiting may require hospital care.
\end{itemize}
