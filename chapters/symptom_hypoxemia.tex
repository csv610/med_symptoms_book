\chapter{Hypoxemia and Hypoxia}

\section*{Definition}
Hypoxemia is an abnormally low level of oxygen in your arterial blood, usually defined as an oxygen saturation below 90--92\% or a partial pressure of oxygen below 80 mmHg at sea level. Hypoxia is a broader term for low oxygen reaching your body's tissues, which can occur even with a normal blood oxygen level if your heart cannot deliver it or your cells cannot use it. In health, oxygen saturation is 95--100\%.

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
Get emergency care for any of the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Oxygen saturation persistently below 90\% at home, or below 88\% if you have COPD and were told your target is lower
  \item Severe breathlessness at rest, struggling to speak in full sentences, or gasping
  \item Blue or gray lips, tongue, or fingertips
  \item Confusion, extreme drowsiness, fainting, or chest pain
  \item Sudden onset of breathlessness with leg pain or swelling -- a possible pulmonary embolism
\end{itemize}

\section*{How It Develops}
Oxygen travels a precise path: you breathe it into the air sacs (alveoli), it moves across the thin membrane into the blood, binds to hemoglobin in red blood cells, and is pumped by the heart to your organs. Hypoxemia develops when any step of that path fails -- the air sacs are filled with fluid (pneumonia, pulmonary edema), the airways are narrowed (asthma, COPD), the membrane is damaged (pulmonary fibrosis), blood bypasses the air sacs (shunt, as in atelectasis), or you are not breathing deeply enough (hypoventilation, which also raises carbon dioxide). Your body first responds by breathing faster and making the heart beat harder. When oxygen falls further, the skin and lips can turn blue (cyanosis), and the brain, heart, and kidneys -- the organs that need the most oxygen -- begin to malfunction, causing confusion, chest discomfort, and fatigue.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lung disease: Pneumonia, asthma, COPD, interstitial lung disease, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)
  \item Heart and circulation: Heart failure and pulmonary edema, pulmonary embolism, and shock
  \item Lung structure: Collapsed lung (pneumothorax), fluid in the chest cavity, and atelectasis
  \item Breathing problems: Hypoventilation from obesity, sleep apnea, sedating drugs, opioids, or neuromuscular disease
  \item Environmental: High altitude, where thin air carries less oxygen
  \item Blood conditions: Severe anemia, in which too few red blood cells carry oxygen
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Shortness of breath and rapid, shallow breathing
  \item Bluish discoloration of the lips, fingers, or skin
  \item Headache (especially in the morning), confusion, or irritability
  \item Fatigue, weakness, and difficulty concentrating
  \item Rapid heart rate and chest tightness or discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{Medical Evaluation}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Pulse oximetry gives an immediate oxygen reading; an arterial blood gas test measures oxygen, carbon dioxide, and acid-base balance directly.
  \item A chest X-ray looks for pneumonia, fluid, or a collapsed lung; CT scanning can detect pulmonary embolism.
  \item An ECG and heart tests check whether heart failure is the cause.
  \item Blood counts reveal anemia, and carbon monoxide poisoning is checked if the history suggests it.
  \item Breathing tests (spirometry) and sleep studies identify asthma, COPD, and sleep-disordered breathing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Supplemental oxygen is prescribed to an individualized target; common adult targets are 94--98\%, or 88--92\% in people at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure.
  \item Bronchodilators and corticosteroids are used for selected obstructive or inflammatory diseases, not for every cause of hypoxemia.
  \item Antibiotics, antivirals, corticosteroids, or other treatment are chosen according to the underlying diagnosis.
  \item Anticoagulants treat pulmonary emboli; diuretics remove fluid in heart failure.
  \item A chest tube re-expands a collapsed lung.
  \item Noninvasive ventilation (CPAP, BiPAP) or mechanical ventilation supports breathing when ventilation fails or oxygen alone is insufficient.
  \item Treating the drivers of oxygen demand -- fever, pain, anemia -- helps the body use oxygen more efficiently.
\end{itemize}


\section*{Self-Care and Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item If you have a chronic lung condition, use a home pulse oximeter to track trends, but never treat readings in isolation -- how you feel matters too.
  \item Follow your lung action plan: use your prescribed inhalers, steroids, and breathing techniques, and avoid smoke, fumes, and other triggers.
  \item Do not take extra oxygen or adjust machine settings on your own; titrate oxygen only under your doctor's guidance.
  \item Sleep upright with pillows if breathlessness is worse lying flat, and see your doctor about sleep apnea testing if you snore heavily and wake gasping.
  \item Stay up to date on vaccinations (flu, pneumonia, COVID-19) to reduce your risk of lung infections.
\end{itemize}
