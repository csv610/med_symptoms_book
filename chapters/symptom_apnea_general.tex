\chapter{Apnea}

\section*{Definition}
Apnea is the temporary cessation of breathing, clinically defined as a pause in airflow for at least 10 seconds (in adults) or more than 20 seconds (in neonates) or any duration accompanied by bradycardia, cyanosis, or desaturation. Central, obstructive, and mixed subtypes exist based on the presence or absence of respiratory effort.

\section*{Pathophysiology}
Obstructive apnea results from pharyngeal collapse due to loss of upper airway dilator muscle tone during sleep, while central apnea arises from failure of the brainstem respiratory centers to generate inspiratory drive. Mixed apnea involves both mechanisms, typically beginning centrally with subsequent obstructive collapse.

\section*{Etiology}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Obstructive sleep apnea:} Obesity, retrognathia, macroglossia, tonsillar hypertrophy, nasal obstruction, neuromuscular disorders
  \item \textbf{Central sleep apnea:} Heart failure (Cheyne-Stokes respiration), stroke, opioid use, high-altitude periodic breathing, brainstem lesions
  \item \textbf{Neonatal apnea:} Prematurity (immature respiratory control), sepsis, intracranial hemorrhage, metabolic disorders, gastroesophageal reflux
  \item \textbf{Transient:} Breath-holding spells (children), voluntary apnea, post-hyperventilation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Indications for Evaluation}
Seek care if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Witnessed apnea episodes with cyanosis, loss of consciousness, or seizure-like activity
  \item Excessive daytime somnolence (Epworth Sleepiness Scale greater than 10) with loud snoring
  \item Gasping or choking sensations during sleep reported by bed partner
  \item Neonatal apnea requiring stimulation or resuscitation
  \item Morning headaches, cognitive impairment, or mood disturbances suggestive of chronic sleep disruption
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Snoring, daytime hypersomnolence, morning headache, nocturia, dry mouth upon waking, irritability, witnessed gasping
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Note:} Untreated obstructive sleep apnea is an independent risk factor for hypertension, atrial fibrillation, stroke, and heart failure.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Weight loss for overweight/obese individuals with sleep apnea (even 10\% reduction can improve symptoms)
  \item Positional therapy: sleep on side rather than supine to reduce airway collapse
  \item Avoid alcohol, sedatives, and hypnotics within 4 hours of bedtime
  \item Elevate head of bed 30 degrees to improve upper airway patency
  \item Nasal saline irrigation and allergy management if nasal congestion contributes to obstruction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnostic Approach}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Polysomnography (in-lab sleep study) or home sleep apnea testing for suspected sleep-disordered breathing
  \item Apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) severity classification: mild 5--15, moderate 15--30, severe greater than 30 events/hour
  \item Oxygen desaturation index and nocturnal oxygen nadir assessment
  \item Electrocardiogram, echocardiogram if suspicion of heart failure as cause of central apnea
  \item Brain imaging (MRI) if central apnea with neurologic signs or suspected brainstem lesion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment / Management Overview}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is first-line therapy for moderate-to-severe obstructive sleep apnea
  \item Mandibular advancement device for mild-to-moderate OSA or CPAP intolerance
  \item Supplemental oxygen for central sleep apnea; adaptive servo-ventilation for Cheyne-Stokes respiration
  \item Upper airway surgery (uvulopalatopharyngoplasty, tonsillectomy) for surgically correctable obstruction
  \item Weight loss surgery (bariatric) for obesity-related OSA refractory to medical therapy
\end{itemize}
