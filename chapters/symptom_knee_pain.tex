\chapter{Knee Pain}

\section*{Definition}
Pain is localized to the knee joint and surrounding structures, one of the most common musculoskeletal complaints affecting mobility and quality of life.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
The knee's weight-bearing role and complex ligamentous/cartilage structures make it vulnerable to acute injury (ligament tears, meniscal tears) and chronic degenerative changes (osteoarthritis). Anterior knee pain suggests patellofemoral pathology; medial/lateral pain suggests meniscal or collateral ligament involvement; diffuse pain suggests inflammatory arthritis.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Osteoarthritis (degenerative joint disease)
  \item Meniscal tear
  \item Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury
  \item Patellofemoral pain syndrome
  \item Patellar tendinopathy (jumper's knee)
  \item Baker's cyst (popliteal cyst)
  \item Rheumatoid arthritis or gout
  \item Iliotibial band syndrome
  \item Prepatellar bursitis (housemaid's knee)
  \item Septic arthritis or osteomyelitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Acute knee injury with inability to bear weight, significant swelling, fever, locking, or instability
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Swelling
  \item Stiffness
  \item Locking or catching
  \item Instability (giving way)
  \item Limping
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Apply the RICE protocol (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) for acute knee injuries or flare-ups, especially after activity.
  \item Use a compression bandage or knee sleeve to provide support and reduce swelling, but avoid wrapping too tightly.
  \item Switch to low-impact activities (swimming, cycling, elliptical) to maintain fitness without aggravating the knee joint.
  \item Over-the-counter NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen or naproxen) can reduce pain and inflammation when taken according to the label instructions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Clinical examination including assessment of gait, range of motion, stability (Lachman, drawer, varus/valgus stress), meniscal provocation (McMurray, Apley), and patellar tracking.
  \item Weight-bearing knee X-rays (AP, lateral, and Rosenberg/sunrise views) to evaluate joint space narrowing, osteophytes, and alignment.
  \item MRI is indicated when ligamentous or meniscal injury is suspected and surgical management is being considered.
  \item Joint aspiration and analysis (cell count, culture, crystals) is performed if septic arthritis or crystal arthropathy (gout/pseudogout) is suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Physical therapy focusing on quadriceps and hamstring strengthening, patellar tracking exercises, and neuromuscular retraining is first-line for most knee conditions.
  \item NSAIDs and acetaminophen provide symptomatic relief; corticosteroid or viscosupplementation (hyaluronic acid) injections may be used for osteoarthritis.
  \item Surgical options include arthroscopic meniscectomy or repair, ligament reconstruction (ACL, PCL), partial or total knee arthroplasty for advanced osteoarthritis.
  \item Septic arthritis requires urgent arthroscopic washout and intravenous antibiotics; gout is treated with NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids depending on chronicity.
  \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
  \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
