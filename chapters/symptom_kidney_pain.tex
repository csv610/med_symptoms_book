\chapter{Kidney Pain}

\section*{Definition}
Pain originating from the kidneys, typically felt in the flank region (costovertebral angle) between the ribs and hip, often radiating to the lower abdomen or groin.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Kidney pain arises from distention of the renal capsule (stretching from inflammation, obstruction, or mass), ureteral colic (smooth muscle spasm from stone passage), or inflammation of renal parenchyma (pyelonephritis). Unlike musculoskeletal back pain, renal pain is typically constant, not positional, and associated with urinary symptoms.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Nephrolithiasis (kidney stones)
  \item Pyelonephritis (kidney infection)
  \item Ureteral obstruction
  \item Renal infarction or thrombosis
  \item Polycystic kidney disease
  \item Renal cell carcinoma
  \item Glomerulonephritis
  \item Hydronephrosis
  \item Papillary necrosis
  \item Perinephric abscess
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Severe flank pain with fever and chills, inability to urinate, blood in urine, or severe unrelenting pain
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Flank pain
  \item Blood in urine (hematuria)
  \item Fever
  \item Nausea and vomiting
  \item Dysuria
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Drink plenty of water (2-3 litres daily unless contraindicated) to promote urine flow and help pass small kidney stones.
  \item Apply a heating pad to the flank area for temporary pain relief while awaiting medical assessment.
  \item Over-the-counter NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen) can provide interim pain relief for renal colic, but seek medical evaluation promptly if pain is severe.
  \item Avoid holding urine — empty your bladder frequently, and strain urine through a filter or gauze to capture any passed stones for analysis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Assessment of flank tenderness, costovertebral angle percussion, and evaluation for fever, abdominal masses, and urinary symptoms.
  \item Urinalysis with microscopy for haematuria, pyuria, and crystals; urine culture if infection is suspected.
  \item Non-contrast CT of the kidneys, ureters, and bladder (CT-KUB) is the gold-standard imaging for suspected nephrolithiasis.
  \item Renal ultrasound is used to assess for hydronephrosis, abscess, or mass, and is preferred in pregnancy and children.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Nephrolithiasis — small stones (<5 mm) are managed conservatively with hydration, analgesia (NSAIDs are first-line for renal colic), and medical expulsive therapy (tamsulosin).
  \item Larger or obstructive stones require urological intervention: extracorporeal shockwave lithotripsy, ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy, or percutaneous nephrolithotomy.
  \item Pyelonephritis is treated with empiric oral or intravenous antibiotics based on local resistance patterns, guided by urine culture results, typically for 7-14 days.
  \item Obstructive uropathy (hydronephrosis) requires urgent decompression via ureteric stent or nephrostomy tube to preserve renal function.
  \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
  \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
