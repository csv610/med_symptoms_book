\chapter{Exophthalmos}

\section*{Definition}
Exophthalmos (proptosis) is pathologic protrusion of the globe from the orbit, quantified on Hertel exophthalmometry as a measurement exceeding 20 mm or a side-to-side asymmetry of more than 2 mm.

\section*{Pathophysiology}
Increased orbital volume, most commonly from inflammatory infiltration (thyroid eye disease), displaces the globe anteriorly within the bony orbit. The expanded tissues comprise extraocular muscle hypertrophy and orbital fat proliferation, with subsequent venous congestion and lymphocytic infiltration.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Thyroid eye disease (Graves' orbitopathy):} Autoimmune-mediated inflammation of orbital tissues, strongly associated with Graves' disease
  \item \textbf{Orbital cellulitis:} Bacterial infection spreading from sinuses (particularly ethmoid)
  \item \textbf{Orbital tumor:} Cavernous hemangioma, meningioma, optic nerve glioma, or lymphoma
  \item \textbf{Orbital pseudotumor:} Idiopathic nonspecific orbital inflammation
  \item \textbf{Vascular lesions:} Carotid-cavernous fistula, orbital varices
  \item \textbf{Traumatic:} Orbital blowout fracture with intraorbital hemorrhage or emphysema
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Unilateral or bilateral eye protrusion noticed by patient or family
  \item New-onset diplopia or restricted eye movement
  \item Orbital pain, eyelid erythema, or fever (suspect orbital cellulitis)
  \item Acute proptosis with vomiting after valsalva (suspect orbital varix)
  \item Vision loss, afferent pupillary defect, or optic disc edema
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Lid retraction and lid lag (von Graefe sign)
  \item Stare appearance (Dalrymple sign)
  \item Diplopia from extraocular muscle restriction
  \item Periorbital edema and chemosis
  \item Exposure keratopathy from incomplete eyelid closure
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} Thyroid eye disease is the most common cause of adult bilateral proptosis; smoking and uncontrolled hyperthyroidism significantly worsen its severity.

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hertel exophthalmometry with serial measurements to track progression
  \item Thyroid function tests (TSH, free T4, T3) and anti-thyroid antibodies (TSI, anti-TPO)
  \item CT or MRI of orbits with contrast for suspected tumor, cellulitis, or vascular lesion
  \item Formal visual field testing and color vision assessment for optic nerve compression
  \item Cover testing and Hess chart for quantification of diplopia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Thyroid eye disease: Achievement of euthyroid state, selenium supplementation (200 mcg daily), smoking cessation
  \item Mild-to-moderate TED: High-dose oral corticosteroids or orbital radiotherapy
  \item Moderate-to-severe TED: Teprotumumab (IGF-1R inhibitor) infusion therapy
  \item Orbital cellulitis: IV broad-spectrum antibiotics with ENT consultation for sinus drainage
  \item Surgical orbital decompression for compressive optic neuropathy, severe proptosis, or cosmesis
  \item Eyelid lengthening (retractor recession) for post-decompression lid retraction
  \item Strabismus surgery once TED is inactive (stable for 6 months)
\end{itemize}
