\chapter{Fever}

\section*{Definition}
Fever is a temporary rise in your body's core temperature, usually in response to an infection or illness. A fever is generally defined as a temperature of 38.0\textdegree{}C (100.4\textdegree{}F) or higher when measured orally, or 38.3\textdegree{}C (100.9\textdegree{}F) rectally. It is different from heat stroke or overheating because with a fever, your body is actively raising its temperature setting.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
When your immune system detects an infection, it releases signaling chemicals that tell your brain's temperature control center to turn up the heat. Your body responds by conserving heat (narrowing blood vessels in your skin and shivering) until it reaches the new temperature. Once the infection is controlled, your body lowers the setting and you cool down through sweating and widening of blood vessels near your skin.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Infections (most common): Viral infections such as colds, flu, COVID-19, and stomach viruses; bacterial infections such as pneumonia, urinary tract infections, strep throat, and ear infections
  \item Inflammatory conditions: Rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and inflammatory bowel disease
  \item Some cancers: Lymphoma, leukemia, and kidney cancer can cause persistent fever
  \item Medication reactions: Certain antibiotics, seizure medications, and vaccines can cause fever
  \item Blood clots: Pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis
  \item Heat-related illness: Heat stroke (different from fever — the body cannot cool itself)
  \item After surgery: Post-operative fever is common and usually temporary
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fever lasts more than 3 days with no clear cause
  \item Temperature reaches 40\textdegree{}C (104\textdegree{}F) or higher
  \item Fever is accompanied by stiff neck, severe headache, confusion, or rash (possible meningitis)
  \item You have trouble breathing, chest pain, or severe abdominal pain
  \item You have a weakened immune system (from chemotherapy, organ transplant, HIV) and develop a fever
  \item You have recently traveled to an area with malaria or other tropical diseases
  \item Your child under 3 months has a fever of 38\textdegree{}C (100.4\textdegree{}F) or higher
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Chills and shivering
  \item Sweating
  \item Headache
  \item Muscle aches
  \item Joint pain
  \item Fatigue
  \item Loss of appetite
  \item Dehydration
  \item Rapid heart rate
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} A fever itself is usually not dangerous, but the underlying cause may need treatment. Persistent or very high fever warrants medical evaluation.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rest and drink plenty of fluids — water, clear broth, or oral rehydration solutions.
  \item Take acetaminophen (Tylenol) or ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin) to reduce fever and body aches, following label dosing instructions.
  \item Dress lightly and keep the room at a comfortable temperature — do not bundle up excessively.
  \item Monitor your temperature every 4-6 hours and track other symptoms to identify worsening trends.
  \item Seek medical attention for fever above 40\textdegree{}C (104\textdegree{}F), fever lasting more than 3 days, or fever with stiff neck, severe headache, rash, or difficulty breathing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will ask how long you have had the fever, how high it has been, and whether it comes and goes or stays constant.
  \item They will ask about other symptoms (chills, sweats, cough, pain) and recent exposures (travel, sick contacts, insect bites).
  \item They will check your vital signs and perform a complete physical examination to look for the source of the fever.
  \item Laboratory tests may include blood counts, blood cultures, urine tests, and inflammatory markers.
  \item Imaging (chest X-ray, ultrasound, CT scan) is based on where your doctor suspects the infection is coming from.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Fever-reducing medications (acetaminophen, ibuprofen) provide symptom relief but do not treat the underlying cause.
  \item Antibiotics are prescribed if a bacterial infection is found or strongly suspected.
  \item Antiviral medications may help for certain viral infections like influenza or COVID-19.
  \item Treating the source — draining an abscess, removing an infected medical device — is essential for some infections.
  \item In most cases, the fever will resolve on its own as your immune system fights off the infection.
\end{itemize}
