\chapter{Abdominal Rigidity}

\section*{Definition}
Abdominal rigidity is the involuntary tensing or hardening of the abdominal muscles. Unlike when you tighten your stomach on purpose, this happens automatically and your muscles stay tight even when you try to relax them. It is a sign of irritation inside the abdomen, often from inflammation of the lining of the abdominal cavity (peritonitis).

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
When the lining of your abdominal cavity (the peritoneum) becomes irritated or inflamed, your body responds by reflexively contracting the abdominal muscles. This is a protective mechanism that splints the area and prevents movement that could worsen the irritation. The affected muscles feel firm or hard to the touch, and any movement of the abdomen — including breathing deeply, coughing, or being examined by a doctor — can cause significant pain.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Peritonitis:} Inflammation of the abdominal lining, most often from a ruptured appendix, perforated ulcer, or diverticulitis
  \item \textbf{Appendicitis:} Inflammation of the appendix, especially if it has ruptured
  \item \textbf{Perforated peptic ulcer:} A stomach or intestinal ulcer that has broken through the wall of the digestive tract
  \item \textbf{Diverticulitis:} Inflammation of small pouches in the colon that has led to a small perforation
  \item \textbf{Ruptured ectopic pregnancy:} A pregnancy growing outside the uterus that has ruptured
  \item \textbf{Ruptured ovarian cyst:} A cyst on the ovary that has burst and causes internal irritation
  \item \textbf{Pancreatitis:} Severe inflammation of the pancreas
  \item \textbf{Abdominal trauma:} Injury to the abdomen that damages internal organs
  \item \textbf{Surgical complications:} Infection or leakage after abdominal surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
Abdominal rigidity is always a medical emergency. Call emergency services or go to the emergency room immediately if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your abdomen feels hard or rigid and you have severe pain
  \item The pain is worse with movement, coughing, or being touched
  \item You have nausea, vomiting, and cannot pass gas or have a bowel movement
  \item You have fever and chills along with the abdominal rigidity
  \item You are breathing rapidly or your heart is racing
  \item You feel dizzy, lightheaded, or faint (signs of internal bleeding or shock)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Severe abdominal pain that worsens with movement
  \item Abdominal tenderness (pain when the abdomen is pressed)
  \item Rebound tenderness (pain when pressure is released suddenly)
  \item Nausea and vomiting
  \item Fever and chills
  \item Bloating or abdominal distension
  \item Inability to pass gas or have a bowel movement
  \item Rapid heart rate or breathing
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} Abdominal rigidity is a sign of a serious medical condition, usually peritonitis or a perforated organ. Do not eat or drink anything until you have been evaluated by a doctor.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Do not eat or drink anything — you may need surgery and should have an empty stomach.
  \item Do not take pain medications, as they can mask symptoms that help your doctor find the cause.
  \item Do not apply heat or pressure to your abdomen.
  \item Go to the nearest emergency room immediately.
  \item Do not wait to see if it gets better — abdominal rigidity is a medical emergency.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will examine your abdomen to check for rigidity, tenderness, and rebound pain.
  \item They will check your vital signs (temperature, heart rate, blood pressure) for signs of infection or shock.
  \item Blood tests will check for infection (elevated white blood cell count) and organ function.
  \item Imaging studies such as CT scan of the abdomen or X-rays may show free air under the diaphragm (a sign of a perforated organ).
  \item Ultrasound may be used to check for gallbladder disease, appendicitis, or ovarian problems.
  \item In some cases, surgery is needed to find and treat the cause.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Surgery is often needed to repair the underlying cause (removing the appendix, repairing a perforated ulcer, draining an abscess).
  \item Intravenous (IV) antibiotics are given to treat or prevent infection.
  \item IV fluids are given to maintain hydration and blood pressure.
  \item Pain medications are given after the diagnosis is made.
  \item Nasogastric tube (a tube through your nose into your stomach) may be placed to decompress the stomach and intestines.
  \item The specific treatment depends on the cause found during evaluation.
\end{itemize}
