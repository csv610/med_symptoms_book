\chapter{Tinnitus (Ringing in the Ears)}

\section*{Definition}
Perception of sound in the absence of an external acoustic stimulus, described as ringing, buzzing, hissing, humming, or clicking, which may be unilateral or bilateral, continuous or intermittent.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Abnormal neural activity in the auditory pathway, from cochlear hair cell damage to central auditory cortex reorganization. Subtypes include objective (arising from vascular or muscular sources near the ear) and subjective (much more common, arising from auditory system dysfunction).

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Noise-induced hearing loss
  \item Age-related hearing loss (presbycusis)
  \item Meniere disease
  \item Otosclerosis
  \item Cerumen impaction (earwax)
  \item Ototoxic medications (aspirin, NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, loop diuretics)
  \item Vascular disorders (pulsatile tinnitus — AV malformations, carotid narrowing)
  \item Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders
  \item Head or neck injury
  \item Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Sudden onset tinnitus, pulsatile tinnitus, tinnitus with hearing loss, dizziness, or focal neurological symptoms
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hearing loss
  \item Vertigo
  \item Ear fullness
  \item Headache
  \item Dizziness
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Avoid exposure to loud noises and use hearing protection (foam earplugs or custom-moulded earplugs) in noisy environments to prevent further cochlear damage.
  \item Use sound enrichment (white noise machine, fan, or smartphone app with nature sounds) at low volume at bedtime to reduce tinnitus perception.
  \item Limit or avoid ototoxic substances: high-dose aspirin, NSAIDs, caffeine, quinine, and alcohol, which can exacerbate tinnitus.
  \item Practise stress management techniques (meditation, deep breathing, yoga) as tinnitus perception is amplified by anxiety and hyperarousal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Comprehensive audiological evaluation: pure-tone audiometry, speech discrimination, tympanometry, and acoustic reflex testing to quantify hearing loss and identify the cochlear or retrocochlear site.
  \item Otoscopic examination to identify cerumen impaction, middle ear effusion, TMJ tenderness, or vascular anomalies; auscultation over the ear for pulsatile tinnitus.
  \item If unilateral tinnitus, sudden hearing loss, or retrocochlear signs are present: MRI with gadolinium of the internal auditory meatus to exclude acoustic neuroma or vascular compression.
  \item Laboratory workup: TSH, CBC, ferritin, vitamin B12, and lipid profile to identify metabolic or haematologic contributors.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Hearing aids for tinnitus associated with hearing loss: amplification improves sound input and reduces central auditory gain that drives tinnitus perception.
  \item Sound therapy (notched music therapy or tinnitus retraining therapy) combined with cognitive-behavioural therapy to reduce tinnitus-related distress.
  \item For severe, refractory tinnitus: a trial of off-label medications (tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs, or gabapentin) with careful monitoring for side effects.
  \item Treat underlying causes: cerumen removal, middle ear surgery for otosclerosis, treatment of TMJ disorder, or vascular intervention for pulsatile tinnitus due to arteriovenous malformation.
\end{itemize}
