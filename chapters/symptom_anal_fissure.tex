\chapter{Anal Fissure}

\section*{Definition}
An anal fissure is a longitudinal tear in the anoderm, extending from the anal verge proximally toward the dentate line, typically in the posterior midline (90\% of cases). An acute fissure is defined as symptomatic for less than 6 weeks, while chronic fissures persist beyond 6--8 weeks.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Passage of hard or large stools stretches the anal canal beyond its capacity, causing a tear in the anoderm. Subsequent internal anal sphincter spasm reduces blood flow to the fissure site (relative ischemia), impairing healing and creating a cycle of spasm, pain, and further injury.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
 \item \textbf{Constipation:} Passage of hard stools, straining during defecation (most common cause)
 \item \textbf{Chronic diarrhea:} Repetitive trauma from frequent liquid stools
 \item \textbf{Childbirth:} Vaginal delivery causing perineal trauma and anal sphincter injury
 \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} Crohn's disease (especially atypical lateral or multiple fissures), ulcerative colitis
 \item \textbf{Other:} Anal intercourse, colonoscopy trauma, tuberculosis, syphilis, HIV, leukemia, sexual abuse
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}

\begin{itemize}
 \item Severe anal pain during and after defecation lasting minutes to hours
 \item Bright red blood on toilet paper or coating the stool surface
 \item Fissure persisting beyond 6--8 weeks despite conservative measures
 \item Multiple fissures or fissures in atypical locations (lateral, anterior) suggesting underlying disease
 \item Associated symptoms: fever, weight loss, abdominal pain, or change in bowel habits
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Sharp anal pain during defecation, post-defecatory throbbing pain, bright red blood per rectum, itching ani
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} A sentinel skin tag at the distal end of a fissure and a hypertrophied papilla proximally indicate chronicity and will generally not heal without intervention.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Sitz baths (warm water, no soap) for 10--15 minutes, 2--4 times daily and after each bowel movement
 \item Stool softeners (docusate 100 mg daily) and bulk-forming laxatives (psyllium) to ensure soft, formed stools
 \item Increase fiber intake (25--30 g/day) and water consumption (8--10 cups/day)
 \item Topical lidocaine 2--5\% gel or ointment applied before bowel movements for pain control
 \item Avoid straining during defecation and prolonged sitting on the toilet
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Visual inspection of the anal canal with gentle separation of the buttocks; no digital exam or anoscopy needed initially
 \item Identify fissure location (posterior midline most common), depth, skin tag, hypertrophied papilla
 \item Anoscopy or proctoscopy for fissures in atypical locations to exclude Crohn disease, malignancy, or infection
 \item Rule out IBD with colonoscopy if atypical features (lateral fissure, severe pain, fistulas) or systemic symptoms present
 \item Biopsy of non-healing or suspicious fissure edges in immunocompromised or at-risk people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Conservative therapy (fiber, hydration, sitz baths, topical lidocaine) for 6--8 weeks heals 60--80\% of acute fissures
 \item Topical calcium channel blockers (nifedipine 0.2--0.5\% ointment or diltiazem 2\% cream) to reduce sphincter spasm
 \item Topical nitroglycerin 0.2--0.4\% ointment (Rectiv) applied every 12 hours for 8 weeks (healing rate 50--70\%)
 \item Botulinum toxin injection (20--50 units) into the internal anal sphincter for chronic fissures (80\% healing)
 \item Lateral internal sphincterotomy (surgical division of the internal sphincter) for refractory cases (90--95\% success, risk of incontinence)
\end{itemize}
