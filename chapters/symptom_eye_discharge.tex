\chapter{Eye Discharge}

\section*{Definition}
Abnormal secretion from the eye ranging from clear and watery to thick, purulent, or mucoid, indicating underlying ocular surface or lacrimal system pathology.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Watery discharge typically accompanies viral conjunctivitis or allergic reaction with increased lacrimation. Purulent (yellow/green) discharge indicates bacterial infection with neutrophil accumulation. Mucoid discharge (stringy, white) suggests allergic conjunctivitis. Chronic discharge may indicate lacrimal duct obstruction or dacryocystitis.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Conjunctivitis (viral, bacterial, or allergic)
  \item Dacryocystitis (lacrimal sac infection)
  \item Nasolacrimal duct obstruction
  \item Keratitis (corneal infection or inflammation)
  \item Stye (hordeolum) or chalazion
  \item Blepharitis (eyelid margin inflammation)
  \item Entropion or ectropion (eyelid malposition)
  \item Foreign body
  \item Contact lens-related infection
  \item Herpes simplex or herpes zoster ophthalmicus
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Eye discharge with vision changes, severe eye pain, photophobia, or in contact lens wearers (risk of corneal ulcer)
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Eye redness
  \item Itchy eyes
  \item Blurred vision
  \item Eye pain
  \item Photophobia
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Gently clean the eyelids and lashes with warm water and a clean cloth to remove crusting.
  \item Apply warm compresses to the affected eye for 5-10 minutes to soothe irritation.
  \item Avoid touching or rubbing the eyes and wash hands frequently to prevent spread.
  \item Discard eye makeup and avoid sharing towels or pillowcases with others.
  \item Remove contact lenses immediately and do not reinsert until symptoms resolve.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Your doctor will examine the eye with a slit lamp to assess discharge character and conjunctival injection.
  \item Visual acuity is checked to rule out corneal involvement.
  \item A swab of the discharge may be sent for culture and sensitivity if bacterial infection is suspected.
  \item Fluorescein staining checks for corneal abrasions or ulceration.
  \item Referral to an ophthalmologist if symptoms are severe, recurrent, or vision-threatening.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Antibiotic eye drops for bacterial conjunctivitis or keratitis.
  \item Antiviral medications (acyclovir) for herpes simplex keratitis.
  \item Antihistamine or mast cell stabilizer eye drops for allergic conjunctivitis.
  \item Warm compresses and lid hygiene for blepharitis-associated discharge.
  \item Lacrimal duct probing or dacryocystorhinostomy for nasolacrimal duct obstruction.
\end{itemize}
