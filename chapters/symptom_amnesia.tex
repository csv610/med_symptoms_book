\chapter{Amnesia}

\section*{Definition}
Amnesia is the partial or complete inability to recall past experiences (retrograde amnesia) or to form new memories (anterograde amnesia) that cannot be attributed to inattention, impaired consciousness, normal forgetting, or malingering. It reflects dysfunction of the medial temporal lobe memory system, particularly the hippocampus and adjacent entorhinal, perirhinal, and parahippocampal cortices.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Declarative memory consolidation depends on the hippocampal circuit receiving input from association cortices via the entorhinal cortex, processing within the dentate gyrus and CA fields, and projecting back through the subiculum. Long-term potentiation at glutamatergic synapses (NMDA receptor-dependent) encodes episodic and semantic information. Bilateral damage to the medial temporal lobes, basal forebrain cholinergic projections, or thalamic nuclei (mammillothalamic tract, anterior thalamus) disrupts encoding or retrieval without affecting working memory, procedural learning, or general cognitive function.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Transient amnestic syndromes: Transient global amnesia (TGA) -- sudden anterograde amnesia lasting 1--24 hours with repetitive questioning and preserved identity, likely due to venous congestion or transient hippocampal ischemia; transient epileptic amnesia (TEA) -- brief amnestic episodes from temporal lobe epilepsy
 \item Traumatic brain injury: Concussion (post-traumatic amnesia, both retrograde and anterograde, duration correlates with injury severity), contusional injury to the temporal poles, diffuse axonal injury
 \item Stroke: Posterior cerebral artery infarction involving the hippocampus and parahippocampus; thalamic stroke (paramedian or tuberothalamic artery territory); anterior choroidal artery infarction
 \item Hypoxic-ischemic injury: Cardiac arrest, carbon monoxide poisoning, strangulation -- hippocampus is selectively vulnerable to hypoxia
 \item Neurodegenerative disease: Alzheimers disease (early and prominent episodic memory loss), frontotemporal dementia (semantic variant presents with loss of word and object meaning), limbic-predominant age-related TDP-43 encephalopathy (LATE)
 \item Infectious: Herpes simplex encephalitis (temporal lobe inflammation), limbic encephalitis (paraneoplastic or autoimmune, anti-GAD, anti-LGI1, anti-NMDA receptor)
 \item Nutritional-toxic: Thiamine deficiency (Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome -- amnesia with confabulation, mammillary body atrophy and diabetes insipidus), chronic alcohol-related cognitive impairment
 \item Drug-induced: Benzodiazepines (especially triazolam, midazolam), anticholinergics (scopolamine, diphenhydramine), Z-drugs (zolpidem), medical cannabis
 \item Surgical: Bilateral medial temporal lobe resection (classic H.M. case), unilateral temporal lobectomy for epilepsy when contralateral hippocampus is compromised
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}

\begin{itemize}
 \item Sudden-onset amnesia lasting more than 24 hours or accompanied by headache, focal neurologic signs, or impaired consciousness
 \item Amnesia after head trauma with loss of consciousness or expanding time of post-traumatic amnesia
 \item Progressive memory decline over weeks to months interfering with daily function
 \item Amnesia with fever, seizure, confusion, or psychiatric symptoms (consider limbic encephalitis)
 \item Memory loss triggered by medication or substance use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Confabulation
 \item Disorientation to time and place (spared orientation to person in most cases)
 \item Repetitive questioning
 \item Apathy
 \item Personality change
 \item Executive dysfunction
 \item Aphasia
 \item Apraxia
 \item Agnosia
 \item Visuospatial impairment
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If you have memory problems without other cognitive issues, your doctor will check for conditions like transient epileptic amnesia or autoimmune limbic encephalitis. Standard brain scans may look normal, so special imaging (FDG-PET) may be needed.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Use memory aids: notebooks, digital voice recorders, smartphone reminders, calendars, and labeled storage to compensate for encoding deficits.
 \item Establish a consistent daily routine with fixed locations for keys, medications, and important objects.
 \item Encourage family members to use simple, direct statements rather than open-ended questions to reduce patient distress.
 \item Avoid alcohol entirely in suspected Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome; ensure adequate thiamine intake in at-risk populations.
 \item Supervise medication adherence and avoid sedative-hypnotics that worsen memory function.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
 \item History including mode of onset (acute vs. insidious), temporal gradient of retrograde amnesia, presence of confabulation, risk factors (alcohol, head trauma, seizures, family history).
 \item Cognitive screening: Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) with emphasis on delayed recall, orientation, and clock drawing.
 \item Laboratory studies: Vitamin B12, thiamine levels, TSH, syphilis serology (VDRL), HIV, and autoimmune/paraneoplastic antibody panel if encephalitis suspected.
 \item Neuroimaging: MRI brain with coronal T2/FLAIR for hippocampal atrophy or signal change; CT head for acute trauma or hemorrhage.
 \item EEG to exclude non-convulsive status epilepticus or temporal lobe epilepsy; FDG-PET if neurodegeneration suspected and MRI is equivocal.
 \item Lumbar puncture with CSF analysis for cell count, protein, glucose, oligoclonal bands, and autoimmune/paraneoplastic antibodies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
 \item Treat reversible causes: IV thiamine for Wernicke encephalopathy (before glucose administration), antiviral therapy for herpes simplex encephalitis (acyclovir), resection of temporal lobe tumors, immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, plasmapheresis) for autoimmune limbic encephalitis.
 \item Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine) provide modest symptomatic benefit in Alzheimers disease and may slow decline; NMDA receptor antagonists (memantine) are added in moderate-to-severe stages.
 \item Discontinue or substitute anticholinergic medications and benzodiazepines when possible.
 \item Cognitive rehabilitation includes external memory aids, errorless learning techniques, and environmental modifications; psychological support for both patient and caregivers is essential for progressive etiologies.
\end{itemize}
