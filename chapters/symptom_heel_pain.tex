\chapter{Heel Pain}

\section*{Definition}
Pain is localized to the posterior or inferior aspect of the heel, commonly caused by mechanical stress on the plantar fascia or Achilles tendon insertion.

\section*{What Happens in Your Body}
Plantar fasciitis results from repetitive microtrauma and degeneration of the plantar fascia origin at the medial calcaneal tuberosity. Achilles tendinopathy involves degeneration of the Achilles tendon fibers from overuse, poor footwear, or biomechanical abnormalities. Heel pain is characteristically worse with the first steps in the morning or after prolonged sitting.

\section*{Causes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Plantar fasciitis (most common cause)
  \item Achilles tendinopathy or insertional tendinitis
  \item Calcaneal stress fracture
  \item Retrocalcaneal bursitis
  \item Heel spur syndrome
  \item Sever disease (calcaneal apophysitis in children)
  \item Fat pad atrophy (especially in elderly)
  \item Tarsal tunnel syndrome
  \item Gout or pseudogout
  \item Seronegative spondyloarthropathy (ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to See a Doctor}
See your doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Symptoms are severe or getting worse over time
  \item Severe heel pain with inability to bear weight, fever, redness, or swelling over the heel
  \item You have other concerning symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Related Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Arch pain
  \item Achilles tendon pain
  \item Ankle pain
  \item Foot numbness
  \item Morning stiffness
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Important:} If this symptom persists, your doctor will check for serious underlying causes.

\section*{Self-Care / Home Management}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Apply ice to the painful area for 15-20 minutes several times daily, especially after aggravating activities.
  \item Perform gentle calf and plantar fascia stretching before taking the first steps in the morning and after prolonged sitting.
  \item Wear supportive footwear with cushioned heels and avoid walking barefoot on hard surfaces; consider over-the-counter heel cups or orthotic inserts.
  \item Take a break from high-impact activities (running, jumping) and switch to low-impact exercise such as swimming or cycling.
\end{itemize}

\section*{How Your Doctor Will Evaluate You}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Clinical examination including palpation of the plantar fascia origin and Achilles insertion, assessment of range of motion, and gait analysis.
  \item Plain weight-bearing radiographs of the foot to identify heel spurs, stress fractures, or arthritic changes.
  \item Ultrasound or MRI is reserved for cases where plantar fascia tear, tendon rupture, or deep infection is suspected.
  \item Referral to a podiatrist, orthopaedic surgeon, or physiotherapist is arranged for persistent pain despite conservative measures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment Options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Physical therapy focused on eccentric calf-stretching exercises and plantar fascia-specific stretching is the cornerstone of treatment for plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendinopathy.
  \item Night splints or strassburg socks maintain the ankle in dorsiflexion during sleep to prevent morning plantar fascia contracture.
  \item Extracorporeal shockwave therapy or corticosteroid injections may be considered for persistent pain after 6-12 weeks of conservative therapy.
  \item Surgery (plantar fascia release or Achilles debridement) is reserved for chronic cases that fail all non-operative measures for at least 6-12 months.
  \item Medications, lifestyle modifications, and physical therapies may be prescribed as appropriate.
  \item Follow-up ensures treatment effectiveness and allows timely adjustments to the care plan.
\end{itemize}
